Psychology & Behavior

Emotions

Fear, Anger, Joy, Grief, Love, and Seven Other Everyday Feelings in Twelve Highlights Each

For centuries, philosophers treated emotions as messy glitches that got in the way of clear thinking. If you jumped at a shadow or lost your temper over a small insult, people assumed your rational mind had temporarily lost control. The same was true when you cried over a movie or blushed until your ears burned.

Modern experiments tell a completely different story. Your feelings are not mistakes. They are fast, automatic survival tools built to handle problems that slow, logical thinking solves too late. If you step toward a coiled snake on a trail, you cannot wait while your conscious brain calmly analyzes the scales and calculates the risk. You need an alarm that freezes your legs in twelve thousandths of a second and asks questions later.

Even feelings that seem painful or embarrassing do real work behind the scenes. Anger makes sure bullies know you will fight back even when fighting costs you money. Disgust started as a reflex to spit out rotten meat and grew into our sense of fairness. Guilt nudges us to fix the friendships we damage. A red blush proves to a room that we are sorry in a way no liar can fake. Looking closely at twelve everyday feelings reveals the clever physical tricks running beneath everything we feel.

    Why Everyday Feelings Are Survival Tools Built Into the Body
  • 1. Fear
    Freezing in Place, False Alarms, and the Woman Who Poked a Haunted-House Monster
    In 1986 at Cornell University Medical Center, neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux showed that sound signals from the ear split into two paths: a slow 24-millisecond high road through the thinking cortex and a fast 12-millisecond shortcut straight to the brain's alarm hub, making you flinch at a curved stick before you even know whether it is a snake.
  • 2. Anger
    Boiling Blood, Costly Payback, and Why Losing Your Temper Protects You From Bullies
    In 1982 in Berlin, economist Werner Güth ran the first Ultimatum Game experiments: when one player offered a greedy 9-to-1 split of real cash, the second player routinely rejected the offer so both walked away with nothing, proving that people gladly pay money out of their own pocket to punish an unfair deal.
  • 3. Joy
    Crinkled Eye Corners, Contagious Smiles, and the Difference Between Wanting and Liking
    In 1862 in Paris, neurologist Guillaume Duchenne used tiny electrical probes on facial muscles to show that a polite fake smile only pulls the corners of the mouth using the cheek muscle, whereas genuine joy also contracts the outer ring of muscle around the eyes—a muscle most people cannot flex on command.
  • 4. Sadness and Grief
    Broken-Heart Adrenaline, Chemical Tears, and Why Bereavement Feels Like Withdrawal
    In 1969, British psychiatrist Colin Murray Parkes tracked 4,486 widowed men after the death of their wives and discovered that during the first six months of grief, their death rate from heart failure and stroke jumped 40 percent above married men of the same age.
  • 5. Love and Attachment
    Prairie Vole Pair Bonds, Mother's Milk Hormones, and Holding a Partner's Hand in Pain
    In 2006 at the University of Virginia, psychologist James Coan placed married women inside an MRI scanner and warned them of a mild electric shock to the ankle: holding a stranger's hand calmed the brain's threat alarm slightly, but holding a loving husband's hand quieted the alarm almost completely.
  • 6. Disgust
    Cockroach Juice, Chocolate Fudge, and How a Rotten-Food Reflex Turned Into Moral Outrage
    In 1986 at the University of Pennsylvania, psychologist Paul Rozin showed that hungry students refused to eat delicious chocolate fudge after watching it molded into the shape of dog feces, and refused to drink apple juice stirred for five seconds with a completely sterile dead cockroach.
  • 7. Guilt and Shame
    The Urge to Hide Versus the Urge to Fix What You Broke
    Across decades of studies starting in the 1990s, psychologist June Tangney proved that shame and guilt are opposite forces: shame says 'I am a bad person' and drives people to hide or lash out in anger, while guilt says 'I made a bad mistake' and drives people to apologize and repair the damage.
  • 8. Envy and Jealousy
    Cucumber Tantrums, Silver-Medal Frowns, and the Pain of Watching a Rival Win
    In 2003 at Emory University, primatologists Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal showed that capuchin monkeys happily ate cucumber slices all day until they saw a neighbor monkey receive a sweet grape for the exact same task—at which point the cucumber-paid monkey hurled the vegetable back at the researcher.
  • 9. Embarrassment
    Why Only Humans Blush and How Turning Red Wins Instant Forgiveness
    In 1872, Charles Darwin called blushing the most peculiar and most human of all expressions because it is the only bodily reaction caused purely by thinking about what other people think of us—and trying to suppress a blush only makes the face burn brighter.
  • 10. Laughter and Play
    Ticklish Rats, Play Bows, and Why Group Laughter Bonds Strangers
    In 1997 at Bowling Green State University, neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp turned on an ultrasonic microphone while tickling laboratory rats and discovered that rats chirp with rapid 50-kilohertz laughter—and chase the researcher's hand begging for more tickles.
  • 11. Nostalgia
    Swiss Mercenary Homesickness, Cold-Room Warmth, and How Old Songs Steady the Mind
    In 1688 in Basel, Swiss physician Johannes Hofer coined the word 'nostalgia' from the Greek words for homecoming and pain to describe a mysterious wasting fever among young Swiss mercenaries fighting in lowland France who fell ill whenever they heard alpine cowherd songs.
  • 12. Gratitude and Awe
    Overview Astronauts, Towering Redwood Trees, and How Feeling Small Makes Us Generous
    In 2015 at the University of California, Berkeley, psychologist Paul Piff and Dacher Keltner stood volunteers inside a grove of 200-foot Tasmanian blue gum eucalyptus trees for sixty seconds and staged an accident where a passerby dropped a handful of pens—and the tree-gazers picked up significantly more pens than volunteers who stared at a tall office building.
