Architectures of Choice · Chapter 11

The Tenth Man

Institutionalizing Dissent from the 1973 Yom Kippur Failure to the Pre-Mortem

At 4:00 a.m. on Saturday, October 6, 1973—the holiest day of the Jewish calendar, Yom Kippur—Zvi Zamir, Director of the Israeli foreign intelligence agency Mossad, telephoned Major General Eli Zeira, head of the Israeli Defense Forces' Directorate of Military Intelligence (Aman), from a safe house in London. Moments earlier, Zamir had concluded a midnight meeting with Ashraf Marwan, the son-in-law of late Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser and Israel's highest-placed espionage asset in Cairo. Marwan had delivered a concrete warning: President Anwar Sadat of Egypt and President Hafez al-Assad of Syria had finalized joint war plans and would launch a two-front invasion across the Suez Canal and the Golan Heights before sunset that day.

For the preceding five months, Israel's intelligence apparatus had lacked neither technical sensors nor raw data. Between May and October 1973, Aman analysts in Tel Aviv had logged over four hundred physical warning indicators along both borders:

  • * Artillery and Bridging Deployment: Egyptian divisions along the west bank of the Suez Canal dismantled defensive earthen ramparts, moved PMP heavy pontoon bridges down to the water's edge, and unpacked high-pressure water cannons designed to blast breaches through Israel's sixty-foot sand wall, the Bar-Lev Line.
  • * Air Defense Umbrellas: Syria and Egypt deployed interlocking belts of Soviet SA-2, SA-3, and mobile SA-6 surface-to-air missile batteries directly up to the 1967 ceasefire lines.
  • * Diplomatic and Family Evacuations: On October 4, forty-eight hours before the attack, Soviet Antonov An-12 transport planes landed in Cairo and Damascus and evacuated every Soviet advisor's wife and child back to Moscow.
  • * Direct Royal Warning: On the evening of September 25, 1973, King Hussein of Jordan flew a helicopter secretly to a Mossad guesthouse north of Tel Aviv to warn Prime Minister Golda Meir in person that the Syrian army on the Golan Heights was in an offensive attack posture, not a training exercise.

Despite possessing every piece of the puzzle, General Zeira and his senior research officers dismissed every indicator right up to October 6, assuring the Israeli cabinet on October 5 that the probability of war was "lower than low." They were trapped inside an institutional orthodoxy known as Ha'Konseptzia ("The Concept"). Formulated after the Six-Day War of 1967, The Concept rested on two rigid axioms: first, that Egypt would never go to war until it acquired long-range Soviet Scud missiles and Sukhoi fighter-bombers capable of striking airfields deep inside Israel; and second, that Syria would never attack without Egypt.

Because the senior Aman leadership held a monopoly on intelligence evaluation and filtered every field report through The Concept, every contradictory fact was rationalized away. Egyptian bridging equipment at the canal was explained as a routine autumn exercise (Tahrir 41); Syrian tank concentrations were labeled a defensive reaction to an air battle on September 13; the evacuation of Soviet families on October 4 was dismissed as a misunderstanding between Cairo and Moscow. When lower-ranking intelligence officers—such as Lieutenant Benjamin Siman-Tov in the Southern Command, who wrote two memoranda on October 1 and October 3 proving that the Egyptian deployment concealed an invasion force—submitted dissenting papers, their superiors suppressed the reports.

At 2:00 p.m. on October 6—four hours earlier than Marwan had forecast—2,000 Egyptian artillery pieces and 240 warplanes opened fire across the Suez Canal while 1,400 Syrian tanks surged across the Golan Heights. Caught with its reserve divisions unmobilized, Israel suffered 2,656 soldiers killed, over 7,200 wounded, 102 aircraft shot down, and 400 tanks destroyed in three weeks of fighting.

In April 1974, the state inquiry led by Supreme Court President Shimon Agranat identified the root failure as the absence of structural dissent inside a monolithic hierarchy. By recommendation of the Agranat Commission, the Israeli military dismantled Aman's monopoly on interpretation and established an independent Red Team unit inside the intelligence directorate named the Control Department, known by the Aramaic Talmudic phrase Ipcha Mistabra—"On the Contrary." Granted unrestricted access to every raw wiretap, satellite photograph, and agent cable, the Ipcha Mistabra unit was bound by an inversion mandate: whenever the primary directorate reached a consensus conclusion, the unit's sole duty was to construct the strongest empirical case that the consensus was wrong, with guaranteed right of direct distribution to the Prime Minister and Chief of Staff.

Fifteen years later, in 1989, the cognitive psychologist Gary Klein and his colleagues Deborah Mitchell and Jay Russo formalized a team analog to Ipcha Mistabra known as prospective hindsight, or the Pre-Mortem. In traditional reviews, asking a team what might go wrong with their plan triggers social conformity; subordinates hesitate to criticize a project the leader has endorsed. In their experiments, Mitchell, Russo, and Klein instructed participants to assume the plan had already been executed and had ended in catastrophe twelve months later, giving everyone five minutes to write down the history of how the disaster occurred. Switching from conditional speculation to retrospective explanation increased the identification of concrete failure modes by 30 percent, breaking the social silence that blinded Tel Aviv in October 1973.
