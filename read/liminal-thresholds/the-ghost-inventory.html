Liminal Thresholds · Chapter 10

The Ghost Inventory

Wolfgang Pauli and the Missing Momentum of Beta Decay (Zurich, 1930)

Under two-body conservation of energy and linear momentum, an unstable atomic nucleus decaying at rest into a daughter nucleus and a single emitted particle must eject that particle at one sharp, discrete kinetic energy. In radioactive alpha decay, where a heavy parent nucleus spits out a helium-4 nucleus, the outgoing alpha particles obey that kinematic rule strictly. Because the recoiling daughter nucleus and the alpha particle fly apart back-to-back with equal and opposite momenta ($\vec{p}_{\alpha} = -\vec{p}_{\text{daughter}}$), the fixed mass difference $\Delta M c^2$ between the initial and final nuclei partitions into a single monoenergetic spectral line.

In 1914, working in Hans Geiger's laboratory in Berlin, British physicist James Chadwick used a magnetic spectrometer and a point counter to measure the momentum distribution of electrons (beta rays) emitted during radioactive beta decay. Expecting to find sharp monoenergetic lines corresponding to specific nuclear transitions, Chadwick discovered instead that beta-decay electrons emerged in a broad, continuous energy smear stretching from near zero up to a sharp maximum endpoint $E_{\text{max}}$, with the average electron carrying less than a third of the transition energy.

For the next thirteen years, nuclear physicists led by Lise Meitner fought to preserve two-body kinematics and energy conservation by arguing that every beta electron was actually ejected from the nucleus at the full discrete energy $E_{\text{max}}$. They proposed that the electrons subsequently lost variable fractions of their energy through secondary collisions or X-ray bremsstrahlung while plowing through the atom's outer electron cloud. In 1927, Charles Drummond Ellis and William Alfred Wooster at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge put that secondary-loss hypothesis to a decisive calorimetric test. They chose radium E (bismuth-210), a pure beta emitter that decays into polonium-210 with a maximum electron endpoint energy of 1.05 mega-electronvolts and a mean electron energy near 0.39 mega-electronvolts. Ellis and Wooster sealed the bismuth-210 source inside a lead calorimeter whose walls were more than a millimeter thick—thick enough to stop every electron as well as any secondary X-rays, gamma rays, or recoiling atomic electrons.

If every decaying nucleus released 1.05 mega-electronvolts and the missing energy was merely being scattered into the surrounding atomic shell, the thick lead block had to trap the entire 1.05 mega-electronvolts per disintegration as heat. Instead, Ellis and Wooster's thermocouples measured an average thermal energy deposition of 0.35 mega-electronvolts per decay, matching the average of Chadwick's continuous electron spectrum within experimental error. More than 0.65 mega-electronvolts of energy per atom was vanishing straight through solid lead without leaving a trace of heat or ionization. The crisis grew so severe that Niels Bohr proposed in his 1930 Faraday Lecture that conservation of energy and momentum broke down inside the atomic nucleus. Under Bohr's hypothesis, energy conservation held only statistically across large averages while failing in individual nuclear decays.

In December 1930, Austrian theorist Wolfgang Pauli at ETH Zurich refused to abandon exact conservation laws and sent an open letter to a physics conference in Tübingen attended by Geiger and Meitner. Pauli proposed that beta decay is not a two-body disintegration at all, but a three-body decay in which the nucleus simultaneously emits an electrically neutral, spin-$1/2$ fermion of tiny rest mass alongside the electron. After James Chadwick discovered the heavy neutral nucleon (the neutron) in 1932, Enrico Fermi in Rome renamed Pauli's light neutral particle the neutrino and in 1933–1934 formulated the quantitative weak-interaction theory of beta decay:

$$n \longrightarrow p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

In Fermi's theory, a neutron inside the nucleus transforms into a proton while creating an electron ($e^-$) and an electron antineutrino ($\bar{\nu}_e$) at a four-fermion contact vertex. Because three particles share the final state rather than two, conservation of momentum no longer fixes the electron's speed. When the antineutrino carries off most of the transition energy $E_{\text{max}}$, the electron trickles out near zero energy; when the antineutrino emerges nearly at rest, the electron takes the full endpoint energy $E_{\text{max}}$. Because the neutrino carries zero electric charge and zero strong color charge, coupling to matter solely through the ultra-short-range weak interaction with a cross-section near $10^{-44}\text{ cm}^2$, it slips through light-years of solid lead unimpeded. In June 1956, Frederick Reines and Clyde Cowan captured Pauli's elusive particle in tanks of water doped with cadmium chloride beside the Savannah River nuclear reactor via inverse beta decay ($\bar{\nu}_e + p \longrightarrow n + e^+$), detecting the delayed coincidence between positron annihilation and neutron capture and wiring a telegram to Zurich confirming that the neutrino existed.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: his dear radioactive ladies and gentlemen, began a one-page typewritten letter mailed from Zurich on December 4, 1930, to a nuclear physics conference in Tübingen. theoretical physicist Wolfgang Pauli had declined to attend the meeting in person because he preferred to attend a charity ballroom dance in Zurich, but his letter proposed a "desperate remedy" to rescue the law of conservation of energy.
Empirical Payoff: Twenty-six years after Pauli's letter, in June 1956, Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines finally captured the particle in tanks of cadmium chloride beside the Savannah River nuclear reactor via inverse beta decay ($\bar{\nu}_e + p \longrightarrow n + e^+$), and wired a telegram to Zurich informing Pauli that his invisible bookkeeper had been found.
