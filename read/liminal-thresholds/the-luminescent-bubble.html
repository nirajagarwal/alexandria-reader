Liminal Thresholds · Chapter 3

The Luminescent Bubble

H. Frenzel, H. Schultes, and the Physics of Acoustic Cavitation (Cologne, 1934)

Unexposed silver-halide photographic plates submerged inside a light-tight tank of developer fluid at the University of Cologne in 1934 emerged covered in dark exposure dots whenever an ultrasonic sonar transducer was energized in the bath. Physicists Hermann Frenzel and H. Schultes had placed the high-frequency piezoelectric quartz transducer into the photographic tank to test whether acoustic agitation could speed up chemical development during marine sonar research. Instead, when they switched off the laboratory lights and let their eyes adapt to the darkness, they saw faint ribbons of blue-violet light glowing inside the water along the pressure nodes of the sound wave.

Sound waves in water are low-energy mechanical vibrations. An audible or ultrasonic pressure wave carries an energy density of roughly $10^{-11}\text{ eV}$ per water molecule, whereas emitting a single photon of visible or ultraviolet light requires 2 to 4 electronvolts. That represents an energy concentration gap of eleven to twelve orders of magnitude. Because thermodynamic laws forbid diffuse low-grade mechanical vibrations from spontaneously pooling into high-energy optical quanta, many physicists initially attributed Frenzel and Schultes's glow to triboluminescence or microscopic electrical discharges across bubbles. For fifty-five years, experimental progress stalled because ultrasonic baths generated chaotic clouds of thousands of cavitation bubbles that grew, collided, and shattered unpredictably in milliseconds. That turbulence smeared any spectroscopic or timing measurement across a noisy ensemble.

In 1989, graduate student Felipe Gaitan and physicist Lawrence Crum at the University of Mississippi solved the experimental impasse by isolating a single cavitary bubble. By driving a spherical quartz flask of degassed water at its resonant acoustic frequency near twenty kilohertz, they created a standing ultrasonic pressure wave whose downward acoustic radiation force balanced the upward buoyancy of a single twenty-micrometer gas bubble. Trapped motionless at the center of the flask, that solitary bubble pulsed in lockstep with the sound field twenty thousand times per second and glowed to the naked eye like a steady blue point star. When Seth Putterman, Brad Barber, and Robert Hiller at the University of California, Los Angeles probed single-bubble sonoluminescence with ultrafast streak cameras and laser light scattering in 1991, they discovered that each optical flash lasted less than a hundred picoseconds. Moreover, consecutive flashes fired with a cycle-to-cycle timing jitter under fifty picoseconds.

The twelve-order-of-magnitude energy concentrator is the nonlinear hydrodynamic collapse of a spherical cavity in an incompressible liquid, governed by the Rayleigh-Plesset equation:

$$R \frac{d^2 R}{dt^2} + \frac{3}{2} \left(\frac{dR}{dt}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{\rho} \left(P_{\text{gas}}(R) - P_{0} - P_{\text{acoustic}}(t) - \frac{4 \mu}{R}\frac{dR}{dt} - \frac{2 \sigma}{R}\right)$$

Here $R(t)$ is the bubble radius, $\rho$ and $\mu$ are the density and viscosity of water, $\sigma$ is surface tension, and $P_{\text{acoustic}}(t)$ is the oscillating sound field. During the tensile rarefaction half-cycle of the sound wave, when the surrounding water is pulled under negative pressure, the microscopic bubble expands from an ambient radius of five micrometers to fifty micrometers. That thousand-fold increase in volume leaves its interior at near-vacuum pressure. When the acoustic wave swings into its positive compression half-cycle, atmospheric and acoustic pressure drive the surrounding water wall inward. Because the converging spherical shell of water focuses kinetic energy into a shrinking surface area ($4 \pi R^2$), the radial acceleration term $\frac{3}{2}(dR/dt)^2$ triggers a runaway implosion. The inward wall velocity surpasses 1,500 meters per second, exceeding Mach 4 relative to the speed of sound inside the gas.

Only when the cavity collapses down to a minimum radius near 0.5 micrometers does the repulsive hard-core van der Waals volume of the trapped gas atoms halt the incoming water wall. Because the final turnaround spans less than a nanosecond, zero heat can escape into the surrounding liquid by thermal conduction, and a converging spherical shock wave reflects off the center of the bubble. Furthermore, whereas diatomic nitrogen and oxygen molecules inside air bubbles dissociate at high temperatures into reactive free radicals that dissolve into the surrounding water over thousands of cycles, chemically inert argon accumulates inside the cavity. Adiabatic shock compression inside the collapsing half-micrometer cavity heats those trapped argon atoms above 20,000 kelvins—nearly four times the surface temperature of the Sun. That extreme heat ionizes the gas core into a dense micro-plasma that emits a broadband flash of thermal bremsstrahlung light lasting under a hundred picoseconds before the next acoustic cycle begins.
