Liminal Thresholds · Chapter 7

The Vanishing Meniscus

Thomas Andrews and the Critical Point Where Liquid and Gas Merge

Inside the chemistry laboratory at Queen's College Belfast during the 1860s, Thomas Andrews turned a steel compression screw into a water-filled cylinder to squeeze pure carbon dioxide above seventy-three atmospheres inside a thick-walled glass capillary tube. For two centuries following Robert Boyle's pneumatic experiments, physicists and chemists had treated liquid and gas as two fundamentally separate, discontinuous states of matter. A liquid possessed cohesion, a fixed volume, high density, and a sharp, curved meniscus boundary separating it from the space above. A gas possessed unconstrained elastic expansion that filled every corner of its vessel.

In the 1820s and 1840s, Michael Faraday at the Royal Institution in London had liquefied chlorine, ammonia, sulfur dioxide, and carbon dioxide by sealing them in bent glass tubes and combining mechanical compression with cooling baths of solid carbon dioxide and ether. Yet six common gases—oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, carbon monoxide, nitric oxide, and methane—defied every attempt at liquefaction. Even when Viennese chemist Johann Natterer compressed oxygen and nitrogen to 3,600 atmospheres in wrought-iron cylinders, not a single drop of liquid condensed. Faced with that wall, chemical orthodoxy divided volatile matter into two permanent classes: condensable vapors and the six permanent gases, which were assumed to possess an intrinsic repulsive elasticity that forbade condensation.

Working in Belfast between 1861 and 1869, Andrews suspected that pressure alone failed because temperature governed a hidden threshold where the distinction between liquid and gas ceased to exist. He designed a precision high-pressure cell in which two heavy glass capillary tubes sat side by side inside sealed copper water jackets filled with water above mercury columns driven by fine-pitch steel screws. One capillary contained pure, dry carbon dioxide. The twin capillary contained dry air whose compression measured the exact hydrostatic pressure from 1 to 110 atmospheres, while external water baths regulated the temperature to within two-hundredths of a degree.

When Andrews compressed carbon dioxide at $13.1^\circ\text{C}$, the gas contracted smoothly until the pressure reached 48.9 atmospheres, at which point a sharp curved meniscus appeared and dense liquid carbon dioxide condensed at the bottom of the tube. At $21.5^\circ\text{C}$, condensation occurred at 60 atmospheres, though the liquid was more expansive and the vapor above it far denser. When Andrews raised the water bath to $30.92^\circ\text{C}$ and cranked the screw past 73 atmospheres, the curved meniscus separating liquid from gas flattened into a horizontal line, lost its refractive contrast, and vanished completely. Inside the capillary sat a single homogeneous fluid phase that was neither liquid nor gas. Whenever Andrews lowered the pressure slightly near that threshold, the column flickered with wavering clouds—critical opalescence caused by density fluctuations reaching optical wavelengths. Furthermore, by heating liquid carbon dioxide above $31^\circ\text{C}$ at 80 atmospheres and then lowering the pressure before cooling it back down, Andrews converted a dense liquid continuously into an ordinary gas without a boiling bubble or meniscus ever forming.

Four years after Andrews delivered his 1869 Bakerian Lecture (On the Continuity of the Gaseous and Liquid States of Matter) to the Royal Society, Dutch physicist Johannes Diderik van der Waals derived the molecular equation of state that explained Andrews's isotherms:

$$\left(P + \frac{a}{V_{m}^2}\right) (V_{m} - b) = R T$$

Whereas the ideal gas law ($P V_m = R T$) treats molecules as volume-free points that never attract one another, van der Waals introduced the excluded molecular volume $b$ and the short-range intermolecular cohesive attraction $a / V_m^2$. Below the critical temperature $T_c = \frac{8a}{27 R b}$, when pressure forces molecules close together, intermolecular attraction wins over thermal agitation and pulls the molecules into a self-cohesive liquid bounded by surface tension. At the critical point ($T_c, P_c = \frac{a}{27 b^2}, V_c = 3b$), the liquid density and vapor density converge to the exact same value, the latent heat of vaporization and surface tension drop to zero, and the first and second volume derivatives of pressure along the isotherm vanish simultaneously. Above the critical temperature where thermal kinetic energy $k_B T$ overwhelms van der Waals cohesion at every density, no amount of pressure can condense a gas. That threshold explained why Faraday and Natterer had failed to liquefy oxygen ($T_c = -118.6^\circ\text{C}$) and nitrogen ($T_c = -146.9^\circ\text{C}$) at room temperature, and guided Louis Cailletet and Raoul Pictet to liquefy the last permanent gases by cascade cooling in 1877.
