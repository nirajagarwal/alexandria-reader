Twelve Materials · Chapter 4

The Delignified Fiber

From Cai Lun's Mulberry Bark to Carl Dahl's Sulfate Wood Pulp

Hold a dry sheet of writing paper up to the light and tear it in half. Along the jagged edge, fuzzy white plant fibers stick out like microscopic bristles. Drop that same sheet into a bucket of water for ten seconds, and it loses almost all its mechanical strength. Unlike woven linen cloth, whose strength comes from spun threads interlocked on a loom, paper contains no weave and requires no glue. Its dry tensile strength relies entirely on intermolecular hydrogen bonds linking bare cellulose microfibrils across less than three-tenths of a nanometer. When water floods a sheet of paper, water molecules wedge themselves between the cellulose chains and break those hydrogen bridges apart.

In 105 CE, at the Eastern Han court in Luoyang, court official Cai Lun presented Emperor He with a standardized papermaking method that replaced heavy bamboo slips and expensive silk scrolls. Cai Lun directed workers to gather the inner bast bark of paper mulberry trees (Broussonetia papyrifera), hemp waste, old rags, and worn fishing nets. They soaked the raw fibers in alkaline wood-ash lye and boiled the mixture. Next, workers pounded the softened mass with wooden mallets until individual cellulose fibrils frayed open, then suspended the pulp in a vat of water. Dipping a woven bamboo screen into the vat lifted a thin, tangled mat of fibers. Pressed and dried in the sun, that wet mat bonded into a crisp writing sheet.

For the next seventeen centuries, global book production was bottlenecked by the supply of discarded linen and cotton rags. Cotton and linen consist of over ninety percent pure cellulose, so beating them in water immediately exposes their hydrogen-bonding hydroxyl ($\text{-OH}$) groups. In the 1840s, German weaver Friedrich Gottlob Keller and Canadian inventor Charles Fenerty tried to break the rag shortage. They pressed barked spruce logs against wet grindstones to make mechanical wood-pulp newsprint. Grinding whole logs unlocked vast forests for cheap newspapers, but it triggered a chemical crisis inside the world's libraries through three distinct molecular mechanisms:

  • * Hydrophobic Lignin Interference: Raw wood contains roughly fifty percent cellulose and twenty-five percent hemicellulose cemented together by twenty-five percent lignin—a rigid, water-repellent three-dimensional aromatic polymer. In mechanically ground wood pulp, residual lignin jackets the cellulose fibers, blocking intermolecular hydrogen bonds and leaving the sheet weak.
  • * Ultraviolet Quinone Yellowing: Exposed to sunlight and atmospheric oxygen, the phenylpropane rings in residual wood lignin rapidly photo-oxidize into light-absorbing ortho-quinones and carboxylic acids, turning white newspaper pages yellow and brittle within days.
  • * Alum-Rosin Acid Hydrolysis ("Slow Fires"): Beginning in the early nineteenth century, papermakers sealed porous wood-pulp sheets against ink feathering by adding rosin soap precipitated with papermaker's alum, or aluminum sulfate ($\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$). In humid library air, aluminum ions react with trace moisture inside the paper to generate free sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), which cleaves the $\beta$-(1$\to$4) glycosidic oxygen bridges of the cellulose backbone and reduces nineteenth- and twentieth-century books to crumbling brown flakes.

Solving both the weakness and the acid self-destruction of wood paper required stripping lignin completely out of wood chips without chopping up the cellulose chains. In 1879 in Danzig, Prussia, German chemist Carl Ferdinand Dahl invented the sulfate pulping process, known globally by the German word Kraft (meaning strength). Dahl cooked wood chips for three hours at $170^\circ\text{C}$ under seven atmospheres of pressure inside a digester filled with "white liquor"—an alkaline solution of sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) and sodium sulfide ($\text{Na}_2\text{S}$).

Inside Dahl's heated pressure vessel, nucleophilic hydrosulfide ions ($\text{HS}^-$) selectively cleave the $\beta$-O-4 aryl-ether bonds holding the lignin net together. Roughly ninety percent of the dark lignin dissolves into the spent liquor while the long cellulose fibers remain intact. Washing away the lignin and buffering modern printing paper with alkaline calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) neutralizes acids before they can attack the cellulose backbone. Whereas nineteenth-century wood-pulp pages sized with acidic alum-rosin lose ninety percent of their folding endurance within eighty years, alkaline Kraft and calcium-carbonate-buffered paper retains its cellulose chain length for more than five centuries.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: Cai Lun's 105 CE Eastern Han court report on macerated mulberry bast and hemp paper in Luoyang, nineteenth-century alum-rosin acid hydrolysis ('slow fires') in mechanical wood-pulp books, and Carl Ferdinand Dahl's 1879 Danzig invention of the alkaline Kraft (sulfate) pulping process.
Empirical Payoff: Whereas nineteenth-century wood-pulp pages sized with acidic alum-rosin lose ninety percent of their folding endurance within eighty years, alkaline Kraft and calcium-carbonate-buffered paper retains its cellulose chain length for more than five centuries.
