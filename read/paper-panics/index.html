Paper Panics

Twelve Financial Manias, Runs, and Liquidity Crashes

Financial markets look like crowds of people arguing over the future. During a panic, however, they behave like a closed hydraulic pipe under sudden pressure. In calm weather, paper claims circulate smoothly as substitutes for scarce cash. Banknotes, broker call loans, futures contracts, and derivative hedges allow thousands of participants to hold claims on the same underlying pool of coin at once. That everyday illusion of liquidity rests on a fragile statistical truce. Market plumbing assumes that claimants will never demand physical settlement at the same hour. It also assumes that falling prices will attract new buyers rather than force existing holders to sell.

A financial panic begins when a small shift in contract design, leverage, or collateral rules breaks that truce. The sign of the feedback loop suddenly reverses. When a forward obligation turns into a penalty-free call option, rational traders bid prices tenfold because they own the upside while leaving the downside behind. Suppose a bank funds twenty-year railway hulls with overnight call money, or an automated hedging algorithm sells shares whenever the index drops. Under those rules, even a minor price dip triggers mechanical liquidation. Each forced sale lowers the market price of collateral. Lower collateral prices trigger fresh margin calls across neighboring balance sheets and drain the remaining pool of buyers.

Understanding a panic requires looking past the human drama on the trading floor. One must inspect the balance-sheet ledger and the contract clause underneath. Across four centuries of paper instruments, liquidity never vanishes by mystery. It disappears when the rules of settlement force every participant toward the same narrow exit door at once.

    The Hydraulic Plumbing of Financial Runs
  • 1. The Winter Option in Haarlem
    Why Turning Tulip Contracts into Penalty-Free Options Sparked the 1637 Frenzy
    November 1636 to February 3, 1637 in Haarlem and Alkmaar, when a Dutch florists' guild proposal to let buyers cancel forward bulb contracts for a 3.5 percent fee converted binding debt into asymmetric call options and drove tulip prices up tenfold in ten weeks.
  • 2. The Rue Quincampoix Printing Press
    John Law's Debt-for-Equity Swap and the Day Paper Pegged to Stock Collapsed
    1716 to May 1720 in Paris, where Scottish monetary theorist John Law merged the Banque Royale with the Mississippi Company to swap two billion livres of French royal war debt into equity—before making the fatal error of pegging paper currency directly to the company's share price.
  • 3. The Bookseller of Exchange Alley
    How Thomas Guy Cashed Out of the South Sea Bubble While Isaac Newton Doubled Down
    April to September 1720 in London's Exchange Alley—John Blunt's South Sea Company lending investors money against their own shares to inflate the stock from £128 to £1,000, while 76-year-old bookseller Thomas Guy sold out in six disciplined tranches between £300 and £600.
  • 4. Black Friday on Lombard Street
    The Collapse of Overend Gurney and the Rule of the Lender of Last Resort
    May 10, 1866 in the City of London, when the giant discount house Overend, Gurney & Co. suspended payment on £11 million of liabilities after hiding rotten railway and steamship loans, prompting Walter Bagehot to formulate the central banking rule of Lombard Street.
  • 5. The Locked Library on Thirty-Sixth Street
    Shadow Trust Companies and How J. P. Morgan Stopped the Run of 1907
    October 21 to November 4, 1907 in Manhattan—after a failed corner on United Copper shares triggered a run on the unregulated Knickerbocker Trust Company, J. Pierpont Morgan audited the Trust Company of America overnight and locked New York's bank presidents inside his library until 4:45 a.m.
  • 6. The Wheelbarrow Mark
    Thirty Paper Mills, Exploding Velocity, and the Overnight Miracle of the Rentenmark
    August to November 15, 1923 in Weimar Berlin, where Reichsbank President Rudolf Havenstein ran 30 paper mills and 1,783 printing presses day and night to print 4.2 trillion marks to the dollar, until Hjalmar Schacht halted central-bank bill discounting and issued the land-backed Rentenmark.
  • 7. The Ten-Percent Margin Trap
    Call-Money Loans and Why Paying Down Debt Deepened the Crash of 1929
    October 24–29, 1929 on the New York Stock Exchange, when $8.5 billion in overnight call-money broker loans hit forced margin liquidation, analyzed by Yale economist Irving Fisher in his 1933 Debt-Deflation Theory after losing $10 million of his own fortune.
  • 8. Silver Rule Seven
    How the Hunt Brothers Cornered Two Hundred Million Ounces and Lost to a Rule Change
    1979 to March 27, 1980 ('Silver Thursday') in Dallas and New York, where Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt cornered one-third of the world's deliverable silver at $50.35 an ounce until COMEX Chairman Michel Marks enacted liquidation-only trading under Silver Rule 7.
  • 9. The Doomsday Machine of Black Monday
    How Automated Portfolio Insurance Turned a Dip into a Twenty-Two Percent Crash
    Monday, October 19, 1987 in Chicago and New York, when computerized portfolio-insurance algorithms designed by Hayne Leland, Mark Rubinstein, and John O'Brien mechanically dumped S&P 500 futures into a falling market and drove the Dow Jones Industrial Average down 508 points.
  • 10. The Broken Pound on Black Wednesday
    George Soros, the Impossible Trinity, and the Ten-Billion-Dollar Wager Against the Bank of England
    September 15–16, 1992 in London and New York, when Stanley Druckenmiller and George Soros shorted $10 billion of British pounds pegged at 2.95 Deutsche Marks in the European Exchange Rate Mechanism, forcing Chancellor Norman Lamont out of the peg by 7:40 p.m.
  • 11. Account Five Eights in Singapore
    Nick Leeson, Short Straddles, and the Earthquake That Brought Down Barings Bank
    July 1992 to February 26, 1995 on the Singapore International Monetary Exchange, where twenty-seven-year-old trader Nick Leeson hid losing Nikkei 225 futures bets inside secret error account 88888 and sold 37,000 short straddles right before the January 17, 1995 Kobe earthquake.
  • 12. The Hot-Potato Algorithm
    How a Volume-Pegged Sell Order Erased a Trillion Dollars in Thirty-Six Minutes
    May 6, 2010 between 2:32 p.m. and 3:08 p.m. Eastern Time, when a Waddell & Reed mutual fund execution algorithm programmed to sell 75,000 E-Mini S&P 500 contracts at nine percent of rolling one-minute volume locked into a high-frequency feedback loop until a five-second CME pause halted trading.
