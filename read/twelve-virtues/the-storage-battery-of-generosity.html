Twelve Virtues · Chapter 6

The Storage Battery of Generosity

Risk-Pooling Networks and How Sharing Meat Beat Spoilage

Between 1974 and 1977, anthropologist Polly Wiessner filled field notebooks in the northwestern Kalahari Desert of Botswana and Namibia. She recorded every ostrich-eggshell bead necklace, iron arrowhead, and cut of gemsbok meat exchanged among the Ju/'hoansi (!Kung) foragers. On the surface, a hunter's life looked precarious. A skilled archer tracking large antelope with poison-tipped arrows succeeded on roughly one out of four days. Even the best marksman often endured two-week dry spells that would leave an isolated nuclear family starving. Yet when a hunter finally brought down a four-hundred-pound eland or kudu in ninety-five-degree desert heat, he faced the opposite problem. Without refrigeration or salt curing, the carcass rotted into maggots within forty-eight hours.

A lone hunter who hoarded three hundred pounds of meat could consume perhaps ten pounds before the remaining two hundred and ninety pounds spoiled into zero value. By contrast, a hunter who distributed cuts of fresh meat across fifteen camp households and maintained formal hxaro gift partnerships with an average of sixteen trading partners living up to a hundred miles away solved the storage problem. He converted perishable protein into a durable social claim. When drought or injury later struck his own territory, he could walk into a partner's camp and draw down caloric credit that had been banked months earlier.

Evolutionary biology and actuarial mathematics explain why generosity beats hoarding whenever individual returns suffer high variance and rapid diminishing marginal utility. In 1964, W. D. Hamilton proved that altruistic sharing among relatives evolves whenever the genetic relatedness $r$ times the recipient's fitness benefit $B$ exceeds the donor's cost $C$ ($rB > C$). Even among unrelated neighbors, pooling independent food harvests across $N$ households slashes the variance of daily caloric intake ($\sigma_{\text{pool}}^2$) directly in proportion to the size of the sharing network:

$$\sigma_{\text{pool}}^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}$$

That same concave utility curve drives generosity in non-human mammals living on the edge of starvation. Between 1978 and 1983, biologist Gerald Wilkinson climbed into hollow trees at Hacienda La Pacifica in Costa Rica to track common vampire bats (Desmodus rotundus). A vampire bat must drink roughly half its body weight in blood every night. If it fails to find cattle for three consecutive nights (about sixty to seventy hours), it starves to death. Because young bats fail to feed on one out of three nights and adults fail on seven percent of nights, Wilkinson observed successful bats regurgitating blood directly into the mouths of hungry roost-mates. They shared with both close kin and unrelated frequent neighbors who reciprocated on later nights.

Because a bat's time-to-starvation follows a concave saturation curve, a full bat donating five percent of its stomach volume sits on the flat plateau of the survival graph. Meanwhile, its starving recipient sits on the steep cliff edge where a few milliliters of blood mean the difference between life and death. Traditional foraging wisdom captured the thermodynamics of this gift economy long before portfolio theory, noting that a wise hunter stores surplus meat not in a hut where it rots, but in the belly of a neighbor. Generosity is the oldest variance-reduction technology on earth. It turns mortal individual volatility into collective survival.

In Wilkinson's Costa Rican hollow-tree trials, a bat that donated a fraction of its blood meal lost seven hours of its seventy-hour starvation reserve while giving a roost-mate thirty-six additional hours of life.
