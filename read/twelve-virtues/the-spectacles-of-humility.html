Twelve Virtues · Chapter 8

The Spectacles of Humility

Washington at Newburgh and the Calibration Curve of Foxes and Hedgehogs

Five hundred furious Continental Army officers crowded onto hard wooden benches inside the newly built timber hall known as the Temple of Virtue at Newburgh, New York, on the afternoon of March 15, 1783. The Revolutionary War was ending, the Continental Congress in Philadelphia was bankrupt, and many officers had gone unpaid for up to five years while their families faced foreclosure at home. Days earlier, an anonymous circular written by Major John Armstrong Jr. had passed through the camp. The pamphlet urged the army to refuse to disband and instead march on Congress at bayonet point—or crown George Washington a military dictator if peace failed to bring back pay.

Washington walked unannounced onto the raised platform through a side door. For ten minutes, he read a prepared speech urging his officers not to stain their eight-year struggle for republican liberty by staging a military coup d'état. Yet the room remained cold and unmoved. Then Washington pulled a crumpled letter from Congressman Joseph Jones out of his coat to prove the civilian government's good faith. He stared at the small handwriting, paused in silence, and reached into his waistcoat pocket for a pair of hinged reading glasses crafted by Philadelphia optician John McAllister. Almost none of his soldiers had ever seen him wear spectacles in public. Looking up at the assembly, he asked his officers to pardon him while he put on his spectacles, explaining quietly that he had not only grown gray, but almost blind, in the service of his country.

In that instant of physical vulnerability, hardened veterans including General Henry Knox and Colonel Samuel Shaw wept openly in their seats. Washington finished reading the letter and walked out of the hall. Within minutes, the officers voted unanimously to reject the mutiny and submit to civilian authority. By refusing the posture of an infallible strongman and admitting his own physical limits, Washington disarmed an armed insurrection that force could never have suppressed.

Epistemic humility is not only a political solvent; it is the single strongest predictor of judgment accuracy in complex environments. Between 1984 and 2003, psychologist Philip Tetlock at the University of California, Berkeley, and the University of Pennsylvania tracked 284 professional political scientists, intelligence analysts, economists, and journalists. Over nearly two decades, those experts logged 27,450 verifiable probability forecasts about elections, wars, currency crises, and GDP shifts. Tetlock classified his participants along Isaiah Berlin's famous axis between hedgehogs and foxes. Hedgehogs were confident thinkers who forced every historical event into one grand ideological law and regularly assigned 90 to 100 percent certainty to their forecasts. Foxes were humble thinkers who drew from messy empirical sources, acknowledged self-doubt, and revised their probability estimates whenever field data contradicted them.

When Tetlock scored the Brier calibration curves after twenty years, the television-famous hedgehogs performed worse than a uniform random-number generator. Their pride prevented them from registering negative feedback, so every wrong prediction only caused them to double down on their original dogma. Humble forecasters treated their own beliefs as testable hypotheses rather than badges of identity. Humility is the cognitive discipline of keeping an internal model open to error correction before reality forces a crash.

Across 27,450 geopolitical predictions in Tetlock's tournament, the most famous pundits who organized every event around one grand theory scored worse than random dart-throwing, while humble forecasters who updated small errors beat the field by thirty percent.

Historical / Experimental Grounding: General George Washington putting on his new reading glasses before mutinous Continental Army officers at Newburgh, New York on March 15, 1783, paired with Philip Tetlock's 1984–2003 forecasting tournament of 284 experts.
