Twelve Virtues · Chapter 9

The Dividend of Mercy

Plow Horses at Appomattox and Why Clemency Ends Insurgencies

Mud-splattered cavalry boots and an unbuttoned private's wool blouse without a sword met spotless gray dress cloth and an engraved presentation saber inside Wilmer McLean's brick parlor at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, at 1:30 p.m. on April 9, 1865. After four years of civil war and more than 620,000 dead soldiers, General Ulysses S. Grant's Union columns had surrounded General Robert E. Lee's starving Army of Northern Virginia. Only that morning, Confederate artillery commander Edward Porter Alexander had urged Lee to reject surrender. Alexander proposed scattering the surviving regiments into the Blue Ridge Mountains to fight a decentralized guerrilla war county by county across the South for another decade.

Had Grant demanded public humiliation, mass imprisonment in northern stockades, or treason hangings—penalties that several Radical Republicans in Washington were loudly demanding—every armed Confederate soldier would have faced a negative-sum payoff for surrendering. In game theory, cutting off an opponent's exit ramp forces them to fight to the last cartridge. That mistake turns a conventional battlefield victory into an unwinnable insurgency. Sitting at a small oval table in McLean's parlor, Grant wrote out terms of surrender in pencil that replaced vengeance with strategic clemency:

  • * Full Combatant Parole: All 28,231 Confederate officers and enlisted men signed printed parole passes and walked home immediately, with an explicit written pledge that they would never be prosecuted by the United States government so long as they obeyed local laws.
  • * Retention of Officer Sidearms: Officers kept their personal swords, pistols, and baggage, preserving the social dignity required for commanders to order their ranks to stack rifles peacefully.
  • * The Spring Plowing Concession: Learning from Lee that Confederate soldiers owned their own mounts rather than riding government horses, Grant instructed his provost marshals to let every man who claimed a horse or mule take the animal home to plant the April corn crop.
  • * Immediate Rations Transfer: Grant ordered twenty-five thousand Union beef and hardtack rations carted directly into the Confederate lines to feed Lee's starving troops that evening.

When Lee read the written draft and noted that his men owned their cavalry and artillery horses, Grant did not amend the formal treaty text. Instead, he issued a verbal order instructing his officers to let every soldier who claimed a horse or mule take the animal home to work his family farm. Lee replied that this single act would do more than anything else to restore harmony among the people.

Mercy is often confused with weakness or the suspension of law. In strategic reality, mercy is a solvent created by asymmetric strength. Only a victor who holds decisive military control can afford to waive the maximum penalty. By waiving that penalty, the victor changes the defeated side's expected value of peace from ruin to survival. Two months later, when President Andrew Johnson's attorney general attempted to indict Lee for treason, Grant walked into the White House and threatened to resign his commission as general-in-chief if the government violated the Appomattox parole. When Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain ordered his Union brigade to snap to the carry-arms salute of honor as John B. Gordon's ragged Confederate infantry marched past to stack their muskets on April 12, the cycle of mutual slaughter broke cleanly.

Giving cornered soldiers a safe exit ramp and a spring harvest dissolved the Army of Northern Virginia in seventy-two hours, preventing the multi-decade guerrilla bushwhacking war that Lincoln and Grant had feared.
