Vaccines · Chapter 12

Prefusion Spike and the Fat Bubble

How Barney Graham, Jason McLellan, and Pieter Cullis Pinned a Shape-Shifting Protein Inside a Lipid Nanoparticle

In 1966 in Washington, D.C., researchers tested a formalin-killed vaccine against Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV)—the leading cause of infant pneumonia—expecting it to work just like Jonas Salk's killed polio shot. Instead, it caused a medical disaster. When vaccinated toddlers caught wild RSV that winter, 80 percent had to be hospitalized with severe lung inflammation, and two children died. For nearly fifty years, nobody could explain why showing the immune system a dead RSV virus made the disease worse instead of better.

Between 2012 and 2016 at the National Institutes of Health Vaccine Research Center in Bethesda, Maryland, viral immunologist Barney Graham and structural biologist Jason McLellan solved that fifty-year mystery using X-ray crystallography. Viruses like RSV and coronaviruses use a spring-loaded surface protein—called the fusion (F) protein on RSV or the Spike protein on coronaviruses—to break into human cells. Before the virus touches a cell, that protein sits folded up like a cocked mousetrap in its "prefusion" shape. The moment it touches a target cell—or gets bumped by formaldehyde or heat in a vaccine vial—the protein snaps into a long, rigid spear in its "postfusion" shape to harpoon the cell membrane.

Graham and McLellan discovered that the 1966 formalin bath had tripped all the viral mousetraps in the vial before injection. Vaccinated children made millions of antibodies against the spent postfusion spear, which could not stop live viruses whose proteins were still in the cocked prefusion shape. By mapping the atomic structure of coronavirus spikes in 2016 and 2017, McLellan and Graham found a simple mechanical fix. Swapping just two amino acids at the hinge of the spike for rigid proline rings (2P) acted like molecular cotter pins. Those two pins bolted the mousetrap in its live prefusion shape so the immune system made fifty times more neutralizing antibodies.

Delivering that pinned-spike blueprint into human cells as messenger RNA (mRNA) required three inventions working together:

  • * The two-proline hinge pin (2P): Designed by McLellan and Graham in 2017 after studying the MERS coronavirus, two proline substitutions lock the spike protein in its live prefusion shape after your muscle cells read the mRNA recipe.
  • * The stealth RNA letter: Discovered by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman at the University of Pennsylvania in 2005, replacing the standard RNA letter uridine with a natural twin called pseudouridine (N1-methylpseudouridine) stops immune sensors from destroying the injected mRNA before your cells can read it.
  • * The pH-switching fat bubble: Engineered over forty years by Pieter Cullis and colleagues in Vancouver, a microscopic droplet made of four fats (a lipid nanoparticle) uses an ionizable fat that is positively charged in acidic mixing vats so it wraps tightly around negatively charged mRNA, turns neutral at pH 7.4 in your blood so it is non-toxic, and flips positive again inside the acidic bubble of a cell to pop the bubble open and release the mRNA.

When Chinese scientists posted the genetic sequence of SARS-CoV-2 online on January 10, 2020, Graham and McLellan slotted the 2P proline pins into the new spike sequence that weekend. Packaged inside modified mRNA and lipid nanoparticles, the first clinical vials arrived at the NIH in forty-two days and achieved 94 to 95 percent efficacy against symptomatic illness across more than 73,000 trial volunteers later that year—while the exact same prefusion-pin discovery finally produced the world's first safe RSV vaccines in 2023.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In 1966 in Washington, D.C., a formalin-killed vaccine against Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) failed catastrophically—hospitalizing 80 percent of infected vaccinees—because formalin caused the virus's surface fusion protein to snap from its live compact 'prefusion' shape into a spent rod before the immune system ever saw it. At the NIH Vaccine Research Center and Dartmouth between 2013 and 2017, Barney Graham and Jason McLellan solved this structural trap for RSV and coronaviruses by substituting two rigid proline amino acids (2P) at the hinge of the spike protein to pin it in its live prefusion shape, then packaged that genetic recipe inside Pieter Cullis's four-lipid nanoparticles.
Empirical Payoff: When the SARS-CoV-2 genetic sequence was posted online on January 10, 2020, inserting the two-proline prefusion pin into the spike recipe and encapsulating the mRNA inside pH-switching lipid nanoparticles produced clinical-grade vaccine vials in forty-two days, achieving 94 to 95 percent efficacy against symptomatic illness in Phase 3 trials of over 73,000 volunteers.
