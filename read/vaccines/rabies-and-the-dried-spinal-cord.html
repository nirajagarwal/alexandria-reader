Rabies and the Dried Spinal Cord

How Louis Pasteur Raced a Lethal Virus on Its Slow Climb Up the Nerves

Active immunization normally fails once a pathogen has breached the body. Primary lymphocyte activation and germinal-center antibody maturation require ten to fourteen days, whereas systemic viral replication unfolds in hours. Rabies is the classic clinical exception to this kinetic barrier. Once the bullet-shaped Rabies lyssavirus reaches the central nervous system and initiates clinical encephalitis—manifesting as pharyngeal spasms, hydrophobia, autonomic instability, and delirium—the infection is virtually 100 percent fatal. Yet between the moment a rabid carnivore sinks its teeth into peripheral muscle and the onset of central neurological symptoms lies an unusually long, silent incubation period lasting anywhere from three weeks to several months.

This latency is governed by the cell biology of peripheral motor and sensory neurons. Rather than disseminating through the bloodstream or lymphatic vessels, rabies virions introduced into deep skeletal muscle bind nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and neural cell adhesion molecules at the neuromuscular junction. After entering the unmyelinated axon terminal by endocytosis, the viral ribonucleoprotein complex latches onto cytoplasmic dynein. Dynein is an ATP-driven molecular motor protein that walks along axonal microtubules from the peripheral synapse toward the cell body in the spinal cord dorsal root ganglia or ventral horn. Because retrograde axonal transport advances at a constant physical velocity of roughly 12 to 100 millimeters per day, the anatomical distance between the bite site and the brain dictates the duration of the asymptomatic window. While sequestered inside the axoplasm of peripheral nerves, the small viral inoculum expresses minimal antigen to surrounding dendritic cells and avoids triggering a systemic immune response.

In the early 1880s at the École Normale Supérieure on the Rue d'Ulm in Paris, Louis Pasteur and physician Émile Roux set out to standardize and attenuate this invisible neurotropic agent without being able to see it under a light microscope or culture it in nutrient broth. Wild street virus isolated from the saliva of rabid dogs produced variable incubation periods in animals ranging from weeks to months. By trephining the skulls of rabbits and serially passaging infected neural tissue directly onto the dura mater of successive rabbits across more than fifty transfers, Pasteur and Roux selected a fixed virus (virus fixe) with a reproducible seven-day incubation period in rabbits and reduced peripheral infectivity.

To transform lethal fixed-virus spinal cords into a graded immunogen, Roux devised an atmospheric desiccation apparatus. Immediately after a rabbit died of fixed rabies, its spinal cord was extracted under sterile conditions and suspended by a silk thread inside a double-mouthed glass flask. The floor of the flask was lined with solid fragments of caustic potash (potassium hydroxide, $\text{KOH}$) kept in a dark room at a constant $23^\circ\text{C}$. Potassium hydroxide served as a powerful desiccant that prevented bacterial putrefaction while dry air and ambient oxygen progressively oxidized the viral lipid envelope and degraded the negative-sense single-stranded RNA genome. A rabbit cord dried for fourteen days lost all residual infectious titer while retaining intact surface glycoprotein antigens (RABV-G). Cords dried for progressively fewer days retained escalating fractions of live virus.

In July 1885, a young Alsatian boy named Joseph Meister was brought to Pasteur's Paris laboratory forty-eight hours after a rabid dog knocked him down and inflicted fourteen deep puncture wounds across his hands, calves, and thighs. Because Pasteur held a doctorate in chemistry rather than a medical license, he consulted neurophysiologist Alfred Vulpian and pediatrician Jacques Grancher. They examined the boy's lacerated limbs and concluded that fatal rabies was inevitable without intervention. That evening, Grancher subcutaneously injected Meister's abdomen with an emulsion of rabbit spinal cord desiccated over potassium hydroxide for fourteen days. Over the ensuing ten days, Meister received thirteen successive inoculations stepping down the desiccation gradient. The regimen progressed from fourteen-day and twelve-day cords displaying non-replicating viral glycoprotein down to a fresh one-day cord loaded with virulent fixed virus. Deposited into subcutaneous tissue rich in dermal dendritic cells, the abundant viral antigen reached Meister's regional lymph nodes immediately. It activated $\text{CD4}^+$ helper T cells and drove B-cell differentiation into plasma cells that flooded his circulation with virus-neutralizing antibodies. Those antibodies intercepted the street virus at peripheral neuromuscular junctions before dynein transport could carry it into his spinal cord. Meister never developed rabies and served for twenty-eight years as gatekeeper of the Pasteur Institute, establishing the post-exposure prophylaxis architecture that today combines cell-culture-inactivated rabies vaccine with human rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated into the wound bed.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In July 1885 in Paris, young Joseph Meister arrived from Alsace at Louis Pasteur and Émile Roux's Rue d'Ulm laboratory with fourteen deep dog bites on his hands, legs, and thighs. Because rabies virus does not race through the bloodstream—it inches up motor nerves from the bite toward the spinal cord and brain at roughly one to two millimeters per hour—Pasteur had a two-to-four-week window to train the boy's immune system after the bite. Pasteur and Roux suspended rabid rabbit spinal cords inside glass flasks over potassium hydroxide crystals for up to fourteen days so dry air gradually killed the virus, then injected Meister with thirteen escalating doses over ten days from fourteen-day-dried cord to fresh one-day cord.
Empirical Payoff: Joseph Meister never developed rabies and later worked for decades as gatekeeper of the Pasteur Institute in Paris, while modern post-exposure rabies treatment pairs four killed-virus shots with ready-made antibodies injected straight into the bite wound to protect nearly 100 percent of patients treated promptly.
