Rabies and the Dried Spinal Cord

How Louis Pasteur Raced a Lethal Virus on Its Slow Climb Up the Nerves

Once a virus has already entered your body, giving a vaccine is almost always too late. Training new antibodies from scratch takes ten to fourteen days, and most viruses multiply through your bloodstream in hours. Rabies is the famous exception to this rule because of the strange, slow way the virus travels. Instead of jumping into your blood after a dog bite, rabies virus enters the tips of motor nerves in the torn muscle. It then crawls upward along nerve fibers toward your spinal cord and brain at a snail's pace of just one to two millimeters per hour.

If a rabid dog bites your hand or leg, the virus can take anywhere from three weeks to three months to reach your brain. Once it arrives in the brain and symptoms start, rabies is nearly 100 percent fatal. While the virus is still creeping up the nerves in your arm or leg, however, it hides inside nerve fibers without waking up your immune system. Louis Pasteur realized that if he injected a vaccine right after a bite, the immune system could build a wall of antibodies in ten days and ambush the virus before it reached the spinal cord.

In the 1880s, microscopes could not see viruses, and nobody knew how to grow them in laboratory dishes. Pasteur and his colleague Émile Roux had to grow rabies inside the nervous systems of rabbits. Passing the virus from rabbit to rabbit produced a predictable strain that always reached the rabbit's brain in seven days. To weaken that lethal virus into a safe training dose, Roux and Pasteur removed the spinal cords of infected rabbits. They hung the cords by threads inside sterile glass flasks with dry potassium hydroxide crystals at the bottom:

  • * Fourteen days of drying: The crystals soaked up moisture while oxygen in the flask slowly damaged the virus. After fourteen days of drying, a rabbit spinal cord lost all ability to cause rabies, leaving behind dead viral proteins that safely showed the immune system the virus's outer shape.
  • * Stepping up the ladder: Each day, a patient received an injection of mashed spinal cord that had dried one day less—twelve days, ten days, seven days—gradually waking up stronger immune defenses as antibodies rose.
  • * The live challenge test: By day ten, the patient could survive an injection of fresh, one-day-dried spinal cord that would kill an unprotected animal, proving their blood was packed with protective antibodies.

In July 1885, a young boy named Joseph Meister arrived in Paris from Alsace at Pasteur's laboratory on Rue d'Ulm. A rabid dog had knocked the boy down and bitten him fourteen times on his hands, legs, and thighs. Because Pasteur was a chemist and not a licensed medical doctor, he asked physicians Alfred Vulpian and Jacques Grancher to examine the boy's deep wounds. Together they agreed the boy was certain to die without treatment. Grancher gave Meister the first injection of fourteen-day-dried rabbit cord that evening. Over ten tense days, Meister received thirteen injections stepping all the way up to fresh rabid cord. His immune system won the race against the nerve-climbing virus. Meister never developed rabies, grew up healthy, and worked for decades as the gatekeeper of the Pasteur Institute in Paris. Today, four painless shots of killed cell-culture rabies virus paired with ready-made antibodies injected straight into the bite wound save nearly 100 percent of patients treated promptly.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In July 1885 in Paris, young Joseph Meister arrived from Alsace at Louis Pasteur and Émile Roux's Rue d'Ulm laboratory with fourteen deep dog bites on his hands, legs, and thighs. Because rabies virus does not race through the bloodstream—it inches up motor nerves from the bite toward the spinal cord and brain at roughly one to two millimeters per hour—Pasteur had a two-to-four-week window to train the boy's immune system after the bite. Pasteur and Roux suspended rabid rabbit spinal cords inside glass flasks over potassium hydroxide crystals for up to fourteen days so dry air gradually killed the virus, then injected Meister with thirteen escalating doses over ten days from fourteen-day-dried cord to fresh one-day cord.
Empirical Payoff: Joseph Meister never developed rabies and later worked for decades as gatekeeper of the Pasteur Institute in Paris, while modern post-exposure rabies treatment pairs four killed-virus shots with ready-made antibodies injected straight into the bite wound to protect nearly 100 percent of patients treated promptly.
