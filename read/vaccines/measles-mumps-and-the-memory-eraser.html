Vaccines · Chapter 6

Measles, Mumps, and the Memory Eraser

How Maurice Hilleman Brewed a Chick-Embryo Trio That Protects Your Old Immune Memories

For decades, doctors tracking childhood survival noticed a puzzling bonus whenever a measles vaccine campaign arrived in a region. When measles shots rolled out across England, the United States, and West Africa, deaths from measles dropped to near zero as expected. At the same time, however, childhood deaths from completely unrelated infections—like bacterial pneumonia, ear infections, and severe diarrhea—also fell by up to 50 percent. Why would a measles shot protect a child against bacteria and other viruses that look nothing like measles?

Before vaccines, measles infected nearly every child before age fifteen. It spreads through the air in tiny droplets that hang in a room for two hours after a sick person leaves, allowing one infected person to spread measles to twelve to eighteen unvaccinated people. In February 1954, pediatricians Thomas Peebles and John Enders isolated the virus in Boston from throat swabs and blood of a schoolboy named David Edmonston during an outbreak at the Fay School. Enders weakened the virus in chick cells to create the first measles vaccine in 1963, though it still gave many children a high fever and rash.

At Merck Laboratories in West Point, Pennsylvania, microbiologist Maurice Hilleman—who grew up raising chickens on a Montana farm—passed the Edmonston strain forty additional times through chick embryo cells kept at a cool 32 degrees Celsius. That extra cooling bred the much gentler Moraten ("More Attenuated Enders") strain licensed in 1968. When his own young daughter Jeryl Lynn woke him in March 1963 with a swollen jaw from mumps, Hilleman drove to his office at midnight to fetch throat swabs. He grew her virus in fertilized hen's eggs to create the Jeryl Lynn mumps vaccine within four years, and in 1971 he combined both with Stanley Plotkin's rubella strain into the single MMR shot:

  • * Measles (Moraten strain): Grown in cool chick embryo cells so it barely causes fever while raising lifelong neutralizing antibodies in over 97 percent of two-dose recipients.
  • * Mumps (Jeryl Lynn strain): Adapted from his daughter's throat swab in hen's eggs until it lost the ability to inflame the brain, salivary glands, or reproductive organs.
  • * Rubella (RA 27/3 strain): Grown by Stanley Plotkin in human cells at 30 degrees Celsius to stop rubella from crossing the placenta in pregnant women and causing blindness, deafness, and heart defects in unborn babies.

The mystery of why measles vaccination saved children from unrelated diseases was finally solved between 2012 and 2019 by immunologists Rik de Swart, Michael Mina, and Stephen Elledge. Unlike ordinary cold viruses that infect nose cells, wild measles virus uses a surface hook (CD150) that sits specifically on your immune system's own memory B-cells and memory T-cells. Those are the veteran cells in your lymph nodes and bone marrow that remember every cold, flu, and bacterial infection you survived since infancy.

In 2019, Mina and Elledge sequenced the entire antibody library in the blood of seventy-seven unvaccinated Dutch children before and two months after a natural measles infection. They proved that wild measles physically hunts down and kills existing memory cells, wiping out between 11 and 73 percent of a child's pre-existing antibodies. For two to three years after surviving measles, a child's immune memory is erased like a formatted hard drive, leaving them defenseless against germs they had already beaten until the MMR vaccine keeps the memory eraser out.
