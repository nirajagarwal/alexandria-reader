Vaccines · Chapter 4

Yellow Fever and the Hundredth Egg

How Max Theiler Walked a Jungle Virus Through Mouse Brains and Chick Embryos

In a Rockefeller Foundation laboratory freezer in New York in 1936 sat a glass vial labeled 17D. Inside floated a strain of yellow fever virus descended from a West African outbreak nine years earlier. It had been passed from dish to dish so many times that it had forgotten how to attack a human liver or ride inside a mosquito.

Yellow fever is a mosquito-borne virus that destroys liver cells, turning the skin yellow with jaundice, causing dark internal bleeding, and killing up to half of severe patients. For centuries, outbreaks emptied port cities from Havana and New Orleans to Lagos and Rio de Janeiro. In June 1927, Rockefeller researchers Adrian Stokes, Johannes Bauer, and N. Paul Hudson working in Lagos, Nigeria received blood drawn from a Ghanaian patient named Asibi who survived a mild fever. They injected Asibi's blood into an Indian rhesus monkey and isolated the yellow fever virus for the first time. The work was perilous. Two months later, Adrian Stokes died in Lagos after catching yellow fever in the lab, one of six Rockefeller scientists killed by the virus they were trying to stop.

Working with monkeys was slow, expensive, and deadly. In 1930, South African physician Max Theiler—working first at Harvard and then at the Rockefeller Institute in New York—found a simpler animal host. He discovered that the Asibi virus multiplied readily when injected into the brains of white laboratory mice. Passing the virus from mouse brain to mouse brain weakened its ability to damage monkey livers. Unfortunately, living in mouse brains taught the virus to attack nerve tissue instead. That side effect made the mouse-adapted virus too dangerous to inject into humans without risking fatal brain inflammation.

To strip out both liver damage and nerve damage at the same time, Theiler and colleague Hugh Smith forced the virus to evolve inside tissue that had neither liver nor brain cells:

  • * Removing the nerve target: In flask series 17D, Theiler and Smith grew the virus in minced chicken embryos from which they had carefully snipped out the brain and spinal cord with scissors before mincing.
  • * Evolution by trade-off: Because a virus carries only a tiny genetic playbook, mutations that helped it multiply rapidly inside headless chick embryo cells over more than a hundred transfers broke the molecular keys it needed to invade human liver cells and nerve cells.
  • Mosquito lock-out: Between the 89th and 114th flask transfers in 1936, the 17D strain lost its toxicity completely. In human volunteers, a tiny under-the-skin dose multiplied just enough to build lifelong antibodies, and it could no longer multiply inside Aedes aegypti* mosquitoes, ensuring mosquitoes could never spread the vaccine virus back into the wild.

How lucky was that exact evolutionary path inside flask 17D? When Theiler and Smith tried to repeat the experiment from scratch with fresh Asibi virus, the virus never mutated the same way again. Modern genetic sequencing later revealed that thirty-two small mutations across the viral genome separate 17D from the original Asibi strain. Every dose of yellow fever vaccine given over the past eighty-five years—more than eight hundred million doses brewed inside fertilized chicken eggs—is a direct descendant of that single 1936 17D flask, earning Max Theiler the 1951 Nobel Prize.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In June 1927 in Lagos, Nigeria, Rockefeller Foundation researchers Adrian Stokes, Johannes Bauer, and N. Paul Hudson isolated yellow fever virus into rhesus monkeys from the blood of a Ghanaian patient named Asibi who survived a mild fever, though Stokes died of laboratory-contracted yellow fever two months later. Starting in 1930 at Harvard and the Rockefeller Institute in New York, Max Theiler and Hugh Smith passed the Asibi virus through mouse brains and then through hundreds of flasks of minced chick embryos whose brains and spinal cords had been surgically removed so the virus could not adapt to nerve tissue.
Empirical Payoff: Between the 89th and 114th subcultures in flask series 17D in 1936, the virus accumulated mutations across its genome that stripped away both its liver toxicity and its ability to multiply inside Aedes aegypti mosquitoes, earning Max Theiler the 1951 Nobel Prize for a single-dose vaccine still grown in fertilized chicken eggs today.
