Vaccines · Chapter 5

Polio and the Killed-Versus-Live Rivalry

How Jonas Salk's Formaldehyde Bath and Albert Sabin's Sugar Cube Stopped Summer Paralysis

Every summer in the mid-twentieth century, swimming pools, beaches, and movie theaters across North America closed as parents dreaded poliomyelitis. Polio is a hardy virus that enters through the mouth and multiplies inside the intestines. In roughly 99 percent of children, it causes only a mild summer fever or no symptoms at all. In about one out of two hundred infections, however, the virus crosses into the spinal cord and destroys the motor nerves that move the legs and chest, leaving children in leg braces or iron-lung breathing tanks.

Early attempts to make a polio vaccine in 1935 using ground-up monkey spinal cords ended in tragedy, paralyzing several children. The breakthrough came in 1949 at Boston Children's Hospital when John Enders, Thomas Weller, and Frederick Robbins proved that poliovirus did not need nerve cells to grow. It multiplied cleanly in glass flasks of skin, muscle, and kidney tissue. Once vats of poliovirus could be grown in monkey kidney cells without dangerous nerve tissue, two fierce rivals took opposite paths to a vaccine.

At the University of Pittsburgh, Jonas Salk believed you did not need to suffer a live infection to gain immunity. Salk grew all three types of poliovirus, filtered the liquid to remove cell clumps, and soaked the virus in a 1-to-4,000 bath of formalin at 37 degrees Celsius. Salk tracked how the concentration of live virus $N(t)$ dropped over time $t$ as formaldehyde cross-linked its outer shell along a first-order exponential curve:

$$N(t) = N_0 e^{-kt}$$

Even after the concentration of detectable live virus hit zero around day six, Salk kept cooking the batch in formaldehyde for another six days. That extra margin drove the mathematical chance of a single surviving virus particle far below one in a million doses while keeping the outer shell intact. Announced by Thomas Francis Jr. on April 12, 1955 after a field trial in 1.8 million schoolchildren, Salk's injected killed shot filled the bloodstream with antibodies (IgG) that blocked poliovirus from ever reaching the spinal cord. (When Cutter Laboratories failed to filter out microscopic cell clumps that same month, live virus hiding inside the clumps survived the formaldehyde bath and paralyzed two hundred children before strict filtration rules fixed the process permanently.)

At the University of Cincinnati, Albert Sabin argued that an injected killed vaccine left a loophole. It protected a child's spinal cord by putting antibodies in the blood, but it did not stop wild poliovirus from multiplying in the child's intestines and passing to neighbors through sewage. Sabin bred weakened live strains of all three polio types that could be swallowed in a drop of pink liquid on a sugar cube. Because Sabin's weakened virus multiplied harmlessly inside the lining of the gut, it trained local antibodies (secretory IgA) right on the intestinal wall. That gut shield slammed the front door shut so vaccinated children could neither get paralyzed nor spread polio to anyone else.

Yet Sabin's live virus carried a tiny risk of mutating back toward paralysis in roughly one out of 2.4 million doses. Since the Global Polio Eradication Initiative began in 1988, combining Sabin's oral drops to stop community outbreaks with Salk's injected shot to lock in individual safety has cut polio paralysis from 350,000 children a year across 125 countries to fewer than a hundred cases in two countries, with wild types 2 and 3 eradicated worldwide.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: After John Enders, Thomas Weller, and Frederick Robbins proved at Boston Children's Hospital in 1949 that poliovirus could be grown in non-nervous tissue, Jonas Salk at the University of Pittsburgh grew all three poliovirus types in monkey kidney cells and soaked the filtered fluid in 1-to-4,000 formalin at 37 degrees Celsius for up to twelve days along a first-order exponential inactivation curve. Tested in 1.8 million schoolchildren by Thomas Francis Jr. in 1954 and announced on April 12, 1955, Salk's injected killed vaccine blocked poliovirus in the bloodstream from reaching motor nerves, while Albert Sabin at the University of Cincinnati bred weakened live strains given on a sugar cube by mouth starting in 1959–1961 to train gut immunity that stopped transmission altogether.
Empirical Payoff: Since the Global Polio Eradication Initiative launched in 1988, combining Sabin's oral drops to break community transmission with Salk's injected shot to eliminate vaccine-derived cases has reduced global paralysis from 350,000 children a year across 125 countries to fewer than a hundred cases in two countries, with wild types 2 and 3 eradicated worldwide.
