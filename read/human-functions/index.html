Physiology & Biomechanics

Human Functions

Sleeping, Breathing, Walking, Running, and Eight Other Daily Acts in Twelve Highlights Each

Everyday actions such as taking a breath, walking across a room, swallowing water, or laughing at a remark occur with such effortless automaticity that they appear simple. That subjective ease is an illusion created by spinal pattern generators, brainstem reflexes, and elastic tissues that hide their mechanical work from conscious awareness. Every routine act of a living human body solves severe physical constraints in fluid dynamics, structural mechanics, acoustics, and thermodynamics.

Consider the physics of moving across a room. At a casual walk, the stiff human leg vaults over the planted foot like an inverted pendulum. That vault exchanges kinetic energy for gravitational potential energy to recycle two-thirds of the work of each step. Increase velocity past two meters per second—a dimensionless Froude number of one-half—and downward gravity can no longer hold the vaulting hip on its arc. Within a single stride, the nervous system abandons pendulum mechanics and switches to a mass-spring bounce, storing elastic strain energy inside the Achilles tendon and plantar arch.

Similar physical thresholds govern every daily act. Speaking coordinates one hundred respiratory, laryngeal, and lingual muscles to five-millisecond tolerances while sculpting a raw glottal buzz into resonant vowel formants. Blinking occludes the retina for forty cumulative minutes every waking day while corollary discharge signals erase the blackout from perception. Stripped of conversational clichés, the ordinary acts of human life stand exposed as precision biological machinery.

    The Hidden Biomechanics and Physiology of Everyday Action
  • 1. Sleeping
    Adenosine Hourglasses, Glymphatic Flushes, and Brainstem Muscle Locks
    Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman attached electrooculogram electrodes to sleeping subjects at the University of Chicago in 1953 and discovered Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep.
  • 2. Breathing
    Negative Pleural Pressure, Alveolar Surfactant, and Medullary Acid Sensors
    Physiologist John Clements proved in 1957 that Type II alveolar cells secrete a dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine surfactant film that slashes air-water surface tension inside the lungs.
  • 3. Walking
    Inverted Pendulums, Froude Transitions, and Spinal Pattern Generators
    Italian physiologist Giovanni Cavagna demonstrated in 1963 that a walking human leg acts as a rigid inverted pendulum that exchanges kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy at every step.
  • 4. Running
    Mass-Spring Mechanics, Tendon Strain Energy, and Endurance Thermoregulation
    Evolutionary biologists Dennis Bramble and Daniel Lieberman demonstrated in 2004 that Homo erectus evolved specialized tendon springs, a nuchal ligament, and enlarged gluteal muscles for endurance running.
  • 5. Seeing and Blinking
    Ballistic Saccades, Tear-Film Lipids, and Neural Blink Suppression
    Biophysicists Denis Baylor, Trevor Lamb, and King-Wai Yau proved in 1979 using microscopic glass suction electrodes that a single retinal rod photoreceptor generates a measurable current from a single photon.
  • 6. Chewing and Swallowing
    Enamel Prism Toughness, Periodontal Mechanoreceptors, and the Pharyngeal Switch
    Physiologist David Julius proved in 1997 that capsaicin from chili peppers binds the heat-gated ion channel TRPV1, triggering thermal pain pathways at room temperature.
  • 7. Speaking
    Myoelastic Vocal Folds, Formant Resonators, and Millisecond Voice Onset
    Janwillem van den Berg proved in 1958 that human vocal folds vibrate passively via myoelastic-aerodynamic coupling rather than active nerve-by-nerve muscle contractions.
  • 8. Laughing and Crying
    Brainstem Vocal Bursts, Duchenne Eye Crinkles, and Emotional Tear Chemistry
    Neuroscientist Robert Provine analyzed 1,200 natural conversations in 1996 and showed that human laughter follows a rigid 210-millisecond acoustic cadence that occurs four times more often after ordinary remarks than after jokes.
  • 9. Sweating and Shivering
    Latent Heat of Vaporization, Hypothalamic Setpoints, and Brown Adipose Thermogenesis
    Sir Thomas Lewis discovered in 1930 that fingers immersed in ice water undergo rhythmic vasodilation every five to ten minutes—the hunting reaction—to prevent frostbite necrosis while conserving core heat.
  • 10. Gripping and Throwing
    Opposable Thumb Torque, Wet-Grip Wrinkles, and the Nine-Thousand-Degree Shoulder Whip
    Neil Roach and Daniel Lieberman proved in 2013 that Homo erectus evolved a mobile waist, low-torsion humerus, and laterally facing glenoid socket two million years ago to store elastic energy for high-speed throwing.
  • 11. Balancing
    Orthogonal Canal Gyroscopes, Otolith Gravity Stones, and the Seven-Millisecond Eye Reflex
    Robert Bárány won the 1914 Nobel Prize for demonstrating how endolymph fluid inertia inside the three orthogonal semicircular canals drives vestibular nystagmus and post-rotatory vertigo.
  • 12. Healing
    von Willebrand Shear Traps, Neutrophil Bleach, and Contact-Inhibited Epithelial Sheets
    George Winter proved in 1962 that keeping a cutaneous wound moist beneath an occlusive dressing doubles the speed of keratinocyte re-epithelialization compared with allowing an exposed dry scab to form.
