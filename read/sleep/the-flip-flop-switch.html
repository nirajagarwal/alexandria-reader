Sleep · Chapter 2

The Flip-Flop Switch

Mutual Inhibition Between the Ventrolateral Preoptic Nucleus and Monoaminergic Arousal Centers

A mammalian brain lingering in a twilight state between sleep and wakefulness cannot forage efficiently or evade predators. In January 1996, Jonathan E. Sherin, Priyattam J. Shiromani, Robert W. McCarley, and Clifford B. Saper at Beth Israel Hospital and Harvard Medical School in Boston identified the neural brake that prevents this perilous ambiguity. Working with Sprague-Dawley rats, the Boston team searched the anterior hypothalamus for cells that fire selectively during slumber. Earlier lesion studies had hinted that the preoptic area promoted rest, yet the exact cellular population remained unmapped. Sherin and his colleagues used immunocytochemistry to stain for Fos, an immediate-early gene protein that accumulates inside metabolically active neurons. When rats slept naturally before tissue fixation, a dense cluster of cells in the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus showed heavy Fos expression. The number of stained neurons scaled in direct proportion to the duration of prior sleep, regardless of circadian phase.

To trace where these sleep-active cells projected, the researchers injected the retrograde tracer cholera toxin B into the tuberomammillary nucleus of the posterior hypothalamus. The tuberomammillary nucleus houses the brain's sole population of histamine-producing neurons, which fire briskly during waking and fall silent during sleep. Dual-label staining revealed that the sleep-active cells in the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus projected directly to the histaminergic core. Subsequent biochemical assays confirmed that these preoptic projection neurons synthesized both gamma-aminobutyric acid and the inhibitory neuropeptide galanin. By releasing these inhibitory neurotransmitters onto histaminergic dendrites, the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus acts as a monosynaptic brake on the ascending arousal system.

Anatomical tracing soon showed that this inhibitory pathway extends beyond histamine neurons to the principal monoaminergic nuclei of the brainstem. Axons from the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus innervate the serotonergic dorsal raphe and the noradrenergic locus coeruleus in the pons. During wakefulness, the locus coeruleus drives cortical vigilance while sending reciprocal noradrenergic projections back to inhibit the preoptic sleep center. When the animal is awake, norepinephrine and serotonin silence the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus, disinhibiting the monoaminergic cores so they maintain their own firing. As homeostatic sleep pressure mounts through extracellular adenosine accumulation, preoptic neurons overcome this monoaminergic suppression and begin discharging. Their release of gamma-aminobutyric acid and galanin shuts down the locus coeruleus and tuberomammillary nucleus, removing the inhibition on the preoptic cluster itself.

Because each side of this circuit inhibits the other, the network operates like an electrical flip-flop switch. Electrical engineers design bistable flip-flop circuits with cross-coupled gates so the system avoids lingering in intermediate voltage states. In the hypothalamus and pons, mutual inhibition creates a sharp threshold that forces the brain to snap cleanly between consciousness and unconsciousness. In May 2000 at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Jun Lu, Mary Ann Greco, Priyattam J. Shiromani, and Clifford B. Saper tested the necessity of the sleep pole using targeted neurotoxins. They delivered bilateral microinjections of ibotenic acid into the rat preoptic area, destroying fiber-sparing cell bodies while leaving passing axons intact. Rats suffering an 80 to 90 percent bilateral loss of ventrolateral preoptic cluster neurons experienced a 50 to 60 percent reduction in non-rapid eye movement sleep time. Total sleep loss averaged 56 percent across weeks of continuous recording, while electroencephalographic delta power dropped by 60 to 70 percent.

A bistable switch that flips too easily, however, becomes unstable whenever minor perturbations strike either pole during the day. To prevent unwanted state transitions during waking activity, roughly 70,000 neurons in the lateral hypothalamus synthesize the excitatory neuropeptides orexin-A and orexin-B, also known as hypocretins. Orexin axons send dense excitatory projections to the locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe, and tuberomammillary nucleus, reinforcing monoaminergic tone without directly inhibiting the preoptic sleep center. By pressing down on the wake side of the circuit, orexin acts as a neurochemical finger holding the toggle in the waking position. In human narcolepsy type 1 patients, postmortem hypothalamic tissue assays reveal an 85 to 95 percent loss of orexin-producing neurons, removing the lateral hypothalamic stabilizer on the flip-flop switch and triggering abrupt daytime intrusions of rapid eye movement sleep and muscle atonia.
