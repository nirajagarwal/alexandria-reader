Elemental Asymmetries · Chapter 9

The Resistive Ceiling

Copper and the Wiedemann-Franz Thermal Dissipation Ratio (Pasadena, 1911)

Two thousand five hundred tons of hard-drawn copper wire, 4.19 millimeters in diameter (No. 8 BWG), stretched across 130,000 wooden poles from New York to San Francisco. In 1911, electrical engineer Frank B. Jewett was supervising the construction of the first transcontinental telephone line for the American Telephone and Telegraph Company (AT&T). With early vacuum tube repeaters still experimental, the engineering challenge was overcoming the resistive power loss of transmitting an electric voice current across 3,400 miles of continental landmass. At an installation cost exceeding $1,000 per mile in 1911 currency, the single two-wire circuit consumed nearly five million pounds of pure copper:

$$R = \rho \cdot \frac{L}{A}$$

Where $\rho$ is the electrical resistivity of the material. Silver possesses the lowest electrical resistivity of any metal at room temperature ($1.59 \times 10^{-8} \ \Omega \cdot \text{m}$), but its high cost prohibits continental-scale infrastructure. Copper ($\text{Cu}$, element number 29) is the industrial cornerstone of the electrical grid, possessing a resistivity of only $1.68 \times 10^{-8} \ \Omega \cdot \text{m}$—less than six percent higher than silver at a fraction of the cost.

Copper's high conductivity stems from its filled $3d$ subshell and single $4s$ valence electron ($[\text{Ar}] \, 3d^{10} \, 4s^1$). In metallic copper, the $4s$ valence electrons are delocalized into a broad conduction band, forming an exceptionally high carrier density ($n = 8.49 \times 10^{28} \text{ electrons/m}^3$). Because the filled $3d$ subshell holds its ten electrons tightly around the nucleus, the copper lattice ions present a small ionic radius, resulting in minimal electron-phonon scattering at room temperature. The mean free path of a conduction electron in pure copper is approximately 39 nanometers—nearly one hundred lattice spacings.

In 1853, Gustav Wiedemann and Rudolf Franz discovered that the ratio of thermal conductivity ($k$) to electrical conductivity ($\sigma$) is nearly identical for all metals at a given temperature, formalized by Ludvig Lorenz:

$$\frac{k}{\sigma} = L \cdot T$$

Where $L$ is the universal Lorenz number:

$$L = \frac{\pi^2}{3} \cdot \left( \frac{k_{B}}{e} \right)^2 = 2.44 \times 10^{-8} \text{ W} \cdot \Omega / \text{K}^2$$

The Wiedemann-Franz law reveals that in copper, heat and electricity are transported by the exact same degenerate Fermi electron gas. This law imposes an unyielding engineering ceiling on modern power grids and microprocessor architecture: Joule heating ($P = I^2 R$) generates thermal energy that must be dissipated, yet any material engineered to conduct electricity efficiently will inevitably conduct heat, and any alloy modified to increase mechanical strength will increase electron scattering and drive up electrical resistance.

Today, global electricity grids, high-voltage substations, electric vehicle traction motors, and data center busbars consume nearly twenty-eight million metric tons of copper annually. As copper ore grades decline globally from historical averages of two percent down to less than 0.5 percent, extracting and refining one ton of copper requires moving over two hundred tons of waste rock and consuming thousands of kilowatt-hours of electrical energy.
