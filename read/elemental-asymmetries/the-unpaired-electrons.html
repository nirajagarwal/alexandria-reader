Elemental Asymmetries · Chapter 5

The Unpaired Electrons

Neodymium and the Coercivity of Rare Earth Permanent Magnets (Tokyo, 1982)

Civil conflict in Zaire's Katanga province in 1978 drove the global price of cobalt up by a factor of ten, threatening to cripple the electronics and automotive industries that relied on samarium-cobalt ($\text{SmCo}_5$) permanent magnets. At Sumitomo Special Metals in Osaka, Japanese metallurgist Masato Sagawa set out to invent a permanent magnet built around abundant iron rather than scarce cobalt.

Iron possesses the highest room-temperature saturation magnetization of any common transition metal, yet pure iron makes a poor permanent magnet because its cubic crystal lattice has low magnetocrystalline anisotropy—allowing magnetic domains to flip and demagnetize easily. To lock the magnetization vector in place, Sagawa needed to alloy iron with a light lanthanide element possessing unquenched orbital angular momentum, plus a small metalloid atom to distort the crystal lattice into a uniaxial tetragonal geometry. In the summer of 1982, he sintered a ternary compound of neodymium, iron, and boron: $\text{Nd}_2\text{Fe}_{14}\text{B}$.

The strength of a permanent magnet is measured by its maximum energy product, $(BH)_{\text{max}}$, representing the magnetic energy density stored between its poles. Neodymium ($Z = 60$) has the ground-state electronic configuration $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^4\,6s^2$, with four unpaired electrons in its $4f$ subshell. Unlike iron's outer $3d$ electrons, whose orbital angular momentum is quenched by neighboring crystal fields ($L \approx 0$), neodymium's $4f$ electrons sit deep inside the atom beneath filled $5s$ and $5p$ electron shells. Their orbital angular momentum ($L = 6$) remains unquenched and couples strongly to the electron spin ($S = 2$) via relativistic spin-orbit coupling:

$$\mathcal{H}_{\text{so}} = \lambda \, \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$$

Inside the 68-atom tetragonal unit cell of $\text{Nd}_2\text{Fe}_{14}\text{B}$, the boron atoms stabilize planar iron layers that provide high magnetic flux density ($B_r$), while the oblate $4f$ electron clouds of the neodymium ions lock the magnetic moment along the crystal's $c$-axis with a massive anisotropy field:

$$H_{A} = \frac{2 K_{1}}{M_{s}} \approx 7.3 \text{ Tesla}$$

Combining iron's flux density with neodymium's coercivity ($H_c$) yielded a maximum energy product exceeding $50\text{ mega-gauss-oersteds}$ ($400\text{ kJ/m}^3$)—enabling the compact permanent-magnet traction motors of modern electric vehicles, offshore wind turbine generators, and hard-disk voice-coil actuators.
