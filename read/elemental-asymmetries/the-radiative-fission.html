The Radiative Fission

Uranium-235 and the Thermal Neutron Cross-Section (Oak Ridge, 1944)

Consuming roughly one percent of the entire electrical generation capacity of the United States in the autumn of 1944, thousands of five-ton electromagnetic calutrons inside the Y-12 plant at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, operated around the clock to separate two isotopes whose masses differ by just 1.26 percent. Inside vacuum tanks wound with 14,000 tons of silver busbars borrowed from the U.S. Treasury bullion depository at West Point, technicians ionized vaporized uranium tetrachloride ($\text{UCl}_4$) and bent the ion beams through magnetic fields to isolate uranium-235 from uranium-238.

Following Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann's discovery of barium fragments in neutron-bombarded uranium in December 1938, Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch calculated that a splitting uranium nucleus converts a fraction of its nuclear binding mass directly into kinetic energy:

$$E = \Delta m \cdot c^2 \approx 200 \text{ MeV per fission}$$

Natural uranium mined from the Earth's crust consists of 99.28 percent $^{238}\text{U}$ and only 0.72 percent $^{235}\text{U}$. In February 1939, Niels Bohr deduced from his liquid-drop nuclear model that abundant $^{238}\text{U}$ cannot sustain a slow-neutron chain reaction; only the rare $^{235}\text{U}$ isotope fissions when struck by thermal neutrons.

The asymmetry arises from nucleon spin-pairing energy in the Bethe-Weizsäcker mass formula ($\delta(A, Z) = + a_p / \sqrt{A}$). A $^{235}\text{U}$ nucleus contains 92 protons and 143 neutrons (an even-odd nucleus). When it absorbs a slow neutron, its 144th neutron pairs up with the 143rd, forming an even-even $^{236}\text{U}^*$ compound nucleus and releasing $6.5\text{ MeV}$ of internal excitation energy—exceeding the $5.3\text{ MeV}$ critical deformation barrier required to split the nucleus and yielding a huge thermal fission cross-section of $\sigma_{\text{fission}}(^{235}\text{U}) \approx 585\text{ barns}$. By contrast, $^{238}\text{U}$ already has 146 paired neutrons; absorbing an unpaired 147th neutron releases only $4.8\text{ MeV}$ of excitation energy, falling short of its $5.5\text{ MeV}$ fission barrier so the nucleus simply captures the neutron to decay into plutonium-239:

$$^{238}\text{U} + n \longrightarrow {}^{239}\text{U} \xrightarrow{\beta^-} {}^{239}\text{Np} \xrightarrow{\beta^-} {}^{239}\text{Pu}$$

Because $^{235}\text{U}$ and $^{238}\text{U}$ share identical electron shells and identical chemical reactions, industrial enrichment had to rely on the three-neutron mass difference in gaseous uranium hexafluoride ($\text{UF}_6$). Under Graham's law of effusion across the porous nickel barriers of the adjacent K-25 gaseous diffusion plant, a single stage enriched the gas by a factor of just:

$$\alpha = \sqrt{ \frac{M(^{238}\text{UF}_{6})}{M(^{235}\text{UF}_{6})} } = \sqrt{ \frac{352}{349} } \approx 1.00429$$

Reaching 90 percent weapons-grade $^{235}\text{U}$ required cascading 2,892 gaseous diffusion stages in series under a single roof covering forty-four acres.
