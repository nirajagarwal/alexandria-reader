The Human Body · Chapter 3

The Eye

Inside-Out Retinas, Single-Photon Rods, and Transparent Living Cells

The vertebrate retina is wired backward inside the eyeball. Incoming light must pass through a dense thicket of ganglion nerves, bipolar cells, and blood vessels before striking the photoreceptors against the back wall. Because the outgoing optic nerve cable must punch back through the retina to reach the brain, every human eye has an unavoidable blind spot.

· · ·

To clear that wiring obstacle at the center of gaze, the retina excavates a 1.5-millimeter pit called the fovea centralis. Covering just two degrees of visual angle—the width of two thumbnails at arm's length—the fovea pushes overlying nerves and capillaries aside. Although it occupies less than 1 percent of retinal area, it claims 50 percent of the visual cortex.

· · ·

Selig Hecht, Simon Shlaer, and Maurice Pirenne proved at Columbia University in 1942 that a dark-adapted human rod cell can detect a single photon of 500-nanometer light. When that lone quantum hits one rhodopsin molecule, an enzymatic G-protein cascade amplifies the hit within milliseconds. The cell closes one million sodium channels, creating a measurable voltage drop.

· · ·

The cornea and crystalline lens are the only living tissues in the body that are optically transparent. The cornea achieves clarity without blood vessels by spacing its collagen fibrils in a uniform lattice separated by 60 nanometers—less than half the wavelength of visible light. Light waves scattered by individual collagen fibers cancel out through destructive interference.

· · ·

Fiber cells at the core of your eye's crystalline lens are among the oldest living cells in your body. Formed during the first trimester of embryonic life, these cells digest their own nuclei, mitochondria, and ribosomes so organelles will not scatter light. They survive for eighty years as clear, quiet bags packed with crystallin proteins.

· · ·

Directly in front of your photoreceptors sits a branching tree of retinal blood vessels, yet you never see them. Your visual cortex runs a temporal filter that erases any shadow remaining stationary on the retina. When an optometrist shines a moving penlight obliquely through your pupil, the shadows shift and leap into view as the Purkinje tree.

· · ·

Vertebrate photoreceptors work backward compared to electronic light sensors. In pitch darkness, ion channels in rod and cone outer segments stay open, pouring out a continuous stream of glutamate neurotransmitter called the dark current. When a flash of light hits the retina, the pigment cascade closes those channels, hyperpolarizing the cell and turning glutamate release off.

· · ·

Even when you stare steadily at a single word, your eyeballs execute involuntary microsaccades and drift sixty times a second. This tremor is essential for sight. When laboratory optics stabilize an image so it never moves across the retinal cells, the photoreceptors adapt within three seconds and the entire scene fades to uniform blank gray.

· · ·

Human color vision relies on three cone pigments tuned to short, medium, and long wavelengths of light. The genes for the red (560 nm) and green (530 nm) pigments sit side-by-side on the X chromosome from a primate gene duplication thirty million years ago. Because men have only one X chromosome, an unequal crossover leaves 8 percent of males red-green colorblind.

· · ·

Neuroscientist David Berson discovered in 2002 that rods and cones are not the only light detectors in the eye. Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells express their own blue-light pigment called melanopsin, peaking at 480 nanometers. These cells contribute nothing to sight, allowing blind patients without rods or cones to synchronize their circadian clocks to dawn.

· · ·

Ultraviolet light and free radicals continuously damage the lipid membranes holding visual pigments. To prevent blindness, the retinal pigment epithelium bites off and digests the outer 10 percent of every rod and cone cell each morning at dawn while fresh disks grow from the base. Your entire outer photoreceptor array is rebuilt every ten days.

· · ·

Contrary to intuition, the internal lens provides only one-third of the eye's focusing power. Two-thirds of total refraction—43 diopters out of 60—occurs at the cornea, driven by the sharp density jump between air ($n = 1.00$) and tears ($n = 1.38$). Opening your eyes underwater erases that air interface and leaves you forty diopters farsighted.
