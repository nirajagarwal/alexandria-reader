The Human Body · Chapter 7

The Gut and Microbiome

Hydrochloric Acid Baths, Five Hundred Million Enteric Neurons, and Microbial Fermentation

Parietal cells in the stomach pump hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) into the lumen against a three-million-to-one concentration gradient. Using $\text{H}^+/\text{K}^+$-ATPase pumps, they drop gastric juice from blood pH 7.4 down to pH 1.5—strong enough to denature tough proteins and kill bacteria. A resting pH of 1.5 is closer to a scavenger vulture's stomach than a typical primate's.

· · ·

Why the muscular stomach does not digest itself was a classic physiological puzzle. Surface foveolar cells secrete a 0.2-millimeter unstirred layer of alkaline mucin gel laced with bicarbonate ions ($\text{HCO}_3^-$). This gel neutralizes acid right at the epithelial wall, while stem cells replace the entire stomach lining every seventy-two hours.

· · ·

Wrapped inside the muscle walls of your esophagus, stomach, and intestines is the enteric nervous system—Meissner's and Auerbach's plexuses. This autonomous mesh holds 500 million neurons, five times as many nerve cells as the human spinal cord. Even if the vagus nerve to the brain is severed, the gut coordinates peristaltic waves on its own.

· · ·

Ninety-five percent of the body's serotonin is synthesized not in the brain, but by enterochromaffin cells in the intestinal lining. Stretching the gut wall releases serotonin to fire local peristaltic reflexes. When swallowed toxins irritate the gut, a massive serotonin dump stimulates vagal fibers to trigger the brainstem's vomiting center.

· · ·

If the six-meter small intestine were a smooth hose, its absorptive surface would measure just 0.5 square meters. Instead, circular folds, finger-like villi, and microscopic brush-border microvilli multiply the surface area sixty-fold to 30 square meters—providing a vast customs dock for rapid nutrient absorption.

· · ·

Biochemist Robert Crane discovered in 1960 that intestinal cells absorb glucose by coupling it to sodium entry via the SGLT1 cotransporter. Even when cholera toxin locks chloride channels open in lethal diarrhea, giving an equimolar solution of salt and glucose activates SGLT1 to pull water across the gut wall—saving over fifty million lives.

· · ·

Human breast milk contains over 200 distinct sugar chains—human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs)—that the infant's own digestive enzymes cannot break down at all. Mothers spend metabolic energy synthesizing HMOs exclusively to feed a single protective microbe, Bifidobacterium infantis, which colonizes the baby's sterile colon and crowds out pathogens.

· · ·

Colonocytes—the epithelial cells lining the large intestine—are the only cells in the human body that do not draw their primary fuel from arterial blood glucose. Instead, they derive 70 percent of their daily ATP by burning butyrate, a four-carbon fatty acid fermented from dietary fiber by anaerobic gut bacteria.

· · ·

While the human genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes—barely more than a millimeter-long roundworm—the 38 trillion bacteria in your colon carry an aggregate catalog of over two million distinct genes. That microbiome synthesizes blood-clotting Vitamin K2, biotin, and secondary bile acids that human cells cannot manufacture.

· · ·

Every twenty-four hours, the salivary glands, stomach, pancreas, liver, and small intestine pour 7 liters of digestive water, acid, and enzymes into the gut lumen—more than your total blood volume. Before stool leaves the body, intestinal sodium pumps reabsorb 98 percent of that fluid, losing only 150 milliliters.

· · ·

Pancreatic acinar cells synthesize trypsin and chymotrypsin powerful enough to dissolve meat in minutes. To prevent the pancreas from digesting itself, it manufactures these enzymes as locked inactive zymogens (trypsinogen) that only snap open when brushed by enteropeptidase on the duodenal wall.

· · ·

When your stomach rumbles loudly two hours after lunch, it is not asking for food; it is running a mechanical street-sweeper. Ninety minutes after the stomach empties, the hormone motilin triggers the Migrating Motor Complex—a forceful peristaltic wave that sweeps undigested fibers and stray bacteria down the small intestine.
