The Human Body · Chapter 5

The Liver

Dual Blood Supplies, Prometheus Regeneration, and Five Hundred Chemical Reactors

The liver is the only visceral organ in the human body capable of full mass regeneration after surgical removal. If a surgeon removes two-thirds of a donor's liver, dormant hepatocytes re-enter the cell cycle within hours. Unlike skin repairs that leave scar tissue, the remaining lobes expand to restore 100 percent of original weight within six weeks.

· · ·

How a regenerating liver knows when to stop growing—the hepatostat—is governed by bile-acid and mechanical signaling. Expanding hepatocytes sense the ratio between liver mass and the bile-acid load arriving from the intestines via the Hippo-YAP pathway. Once functional cell mass matches metabolic demand, cell division halts automatically.

· · ·

Other organs are fed solely by high-pressure arterial blood, but the liver runs on a dual blood supply. Only 25 percent arrives through the hepatic artery; 75 percent flows in at low venous pressure ($8\text{ mmHg}$) through the portal vein from the gut and spleen. Every absorbed nutrient or toxin must pass this customs checkpoint first.

· · ·

If a foreign toxin or drug is fat-soluble, the kidneys cannot excrete it because lipid molecules diffuse back across renal tubules into the blood. Liver cytochrome P450 enzymes run a two-stage assembly line: Phase I oxidizes the molecule to expose a reactive handle, and Phase II bolts on a water-soluble tag for urinary excretion.

· · ·

A single mutation in the liver enzyme CYP2D6 explains why codeine prescriptions fail or poison different patients. Codeine has almost zero activity at opioid receptors; it is an inactive prodrug that CYP2D6 must demethylate into morphine in the liver. Eight percent of Europeans lack functional CYP2D6 and get zero pain relief, while rapid metabolizers risk morphine overdose.

· · ·

Every second, macrophages lining the liver's channels—Kupffer cells—work alongside the spleen to dismantle two million senescent red blood cells. The liver salvages the iron atoms for bone marrow recycling and cracks open the toxic porphyrin ring. That ring becomes green biliverdin and then yellow bilirubin, giving bile its pigment.

· · ·

Your brain burns 5 grams of glucose an hour around the clock, yet blood sugar does not crash during overnight sleep. The liver acts as a glucose buffer, storing 100 grams of branched glycogen chains that it clips into blood sugar overnight. After eighteen hours of fasting, hepatocytes synthesize fresh glucose from amino acids and glycerol.

· · ·

When fasting extends past three days, burning muscle protein into glucose would rapidly destroy the heart and diaphragm. The liver spares muscle by slicing fatty acids into water-soluble ketone bodies—acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate. Ketones cross the blood-brain barrier freely and supply two-thirds of the brain's energy.

· · ·

Hepatocytes synthesize 12 grams of serum albumin every day—half of all protein in blood plasma. Because albumin molecules are too large and negatively charged to escape capillaries, they act as an osmotic sponge inside vessels. This 25-millimeter-of-mercury oncotic pressure keeps water from leaking into the belly and ankles.

· · ·

Drinking grapefruit juice alongside common statins or blood-pressure pills can trigger a dangerous overdose. Grapefruit contains furanocoumarins that bind intestinal and hepatic CYP3A4 enzymes, destroying them via suicide inhibition. Until the liver synthesizes fresh enzyme copies seventy-two hours later, up to five times the normal dose floods the blood.

· · ·

Acetaminophen is harmless at therapeutic doses because 90 percent is conjugated with sulfate or glucuronide for excretion. The remaining 10 percent is oxidized by CYP2E1 into a reactive toxin called NAPQI, which glutathione immediately neutralizes. In an overdose, glutathione reserves run out and NAPQI shreds hepatocyte proteins.

· · ·

Running more than 500 simultaneous chemical pathways, the liver generates more heat per gram than any resting tissue except brown fat. Operating at roughly 38.5 degrees Celsius, a degree above oral temperature, the liver acts as the body's central furnace, warming venous blood right before it enters the heart.
