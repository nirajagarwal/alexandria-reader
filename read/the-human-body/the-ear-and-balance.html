The Human Body · Chapter 9

The Ear and Balance

Levered Ossicles, Sub-Nanometer Hair Bundles, and Three Orthogonal Gyroscopes

Because 99.9 percent of airborne sound waves bounce straight off water due to acoustic impedance mismatch, the middle ear acts as a mechanical impedance transformer. Funneling force from the 55-square-millimeter eardrum onto the 3.2-square-millimeter stapes bone via a 1.3-to-1 lever arm amplifies sound pressure twenty-two-fold before entering the fluid-filled cochlea.

· · ·

At the quietest threshold of human hearing—zero decibels SPL, roughly a mosquito three meters away—the physical movement of the eardrum is extraordinarily small. Laser interferometry confirms that the eardrum and cochlear stereocilia vibrate back and forth by less than 1 picometer ($10^{-12}\text{ m}$)—one-hundredth the diameter of a single hydrogen atom.

· · ·

Inner-ear hair cells do not use slow chemical second-messenger cascades like retinal photoreceptors. Instead, fine protein wires called tip links stretch from the top of each shorter stereocilium directly to a spring-loaded trapdoor ion channel on its taller neighbor, snapping open within 10 microseconds when sound bends the bundle.

· · ·

Biophysicist Georg von Békésy proved in 1948 that the basilar membrane inside the coiled cochlea is a mechanical Fourier frequency analyzer. Narrow and stiff at the base (resonating at 20,000 Hertz) and wide and floppy at the apex (resonating at 20 Hertz), it sorts complex musical chords into physical places before a single nerve fires.

· · ·

Physicist David Kemp placed a tiny microphone inside a quiet ear canal in 1978 and discovered otoacoustic emissions—healthy ears actually emit faint sounds of their own. Outer hair cells packed with the motor protein prestin contract and elongate at kilohertz speeds, acting as an active piezoelectric amplifier on the basilar membrane.

· · ·

Your recorded voice sounds high and thin to you because speaking vibrates the bones of your skull directly. That internal bone conduction adds warm low-frequency bass resonance to your cochlea that airborne sound waves reaching a microphone or another listener's ears never carry.

· · ·

Within 50 milliseconds of a loud gunshot—or right before you shout—the brainstem triggers the acoustic stapedius reflex. Contracting the tiny 1-millimeter stapedius muscle stiffens the stapes bone and dampens low-frequency sound transmission by 20 decibels, protecting cochlear hair cells and keeping your own speech from deafening you.

· · ·

Carved into the dense temporal bone behind each ear are three fluid-filled semicircular canals oriented at ninety degrees to one another for pitch, roll, and yaw. Whenever your head turns in any plane, heavy endolymph fluid lags behind due to inertia and deflects a cupula sail, acting as a three-axis gyroscope in pitch darkness.

· · ·

The Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex (VOR) is the fastest sensorimotor loop in the human body. A three-neuron arc from the semicircular canals to the eye muscles rotates your eyeballs in the exact opposite direction of a head turn with a latency of just 7 milliseconds—keeping street signs crystal clear while you jog.

· · ·

To detect linear gravity and elevator acceleration rather than rotation, the utricle and saccule rest microscopic calcium carbonate stones (otoconia, $\rho = 2.71\text{ g/cm}^3$) atop a gelatinous hair-cell bed. Because the stones are three times denser than fluid, head tilt or linear motion shears them across the hair cells.

· · ·

Spinning in an office chair and suddenly stopping makes the room appear to spin violently the other way. During a steady spin, endolymph fluid catches up to the canal walls; when your skull halts, the fluid's momentum keeps swirling forward, bending the cupula flap backward as if you were spinning in reverse.

· · ·

Unlike birds and fish, which regenerate damaged auditory hair cells throughout life, mammals permanently lose hair-cell mitosis inside the Organ of Corti before birth. Each of the 15,000 hair cells per human ear sheared off by loud concert speakers or aging is gone forever, leaving a permanent gap in your hearing.
