The Human Body · Chapter 12

Sleep and Circadian Time

Twenty-Thousand Master Clock Neurons, Adenosine Hourglasses, and REM Paralysis

French geologist Michel Siffre descended 130 meters into the pitch-black Scarasson glacier cave in 1962 and lived for two months without a watch, calendar, or sunlight. His sleep-wake cycle did not dissolve into chaos, drifting near 24 hours and 30 minutes, while Charles Czeisler's 1999 Harvard forced-desynchrony trials pinned the intrinsic human pacemaker at 24 hours and 11 minutes.

· · ·

Geneticists Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael Young discovered that nearly every cell in your liver, skin, and heart runs its own 24-hour molecular clock via a transcription-translation feedback loop. Each morning, CLOCK and BMAL1 proteins switch on PER and CRY genes; across the day, PER and CRY proteins accumulate and enter the nucleus at night to shut off their own creators.

· · ·

To keep thirty-seven trillion cellular clocks from drifting out of phase like an unconducted orchestra, a pinhead cluster of 20,000 hypothalamic neurons—the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)—sits directly above the optic chiasm. Every morning, retinal melanopsin cells report 480-nanometer dawn light to the SCN, which broadcasts a synchronizing cortisol and temperature wave across the body.

· · ·

Pharmacologist Alexander Borbély showed in 1982 that sleepiness is governed by the gap between two independent oscillators: Process C and Process S. Process C is the 24-hour sinusoidal circadian wave from the SCN. Process S is a linear homeostatic hourglass that builds up sleep pressure every hour you stay awake and drains exponentially during deep slow-wave sleep.

· · ·

The chemical sand inside that Process S waking hourglass is extracellular adenosine—the stripped core of spent ATP energy molecules—which accumulates in the basal forebrain across sixteen hours of waking thought. Caffeine wakes you up by wedging into A1 and A2A adenosine receptors without activating them, blinding your brain to how full its exhaustion hourglass actually is.

· · ·

Anyone who has pulled an all-nighter knows the strange "second wind" of alertness that arrives around 10 a.m. the next morning. Even though your homeostatic adenosine pressure (Process S) is at an all-time high after twenty-six hours awake, your circadian master clock (Process C) fires its scheduled morning wakefulness surge regardless of whether you slept.

· · ·

During Rapid Eye Movement (REM) dreaming sleep, your visual, motor, and emotional cortex fire as intensely as when you are wide awake. You do not act out your dreams because brainstem nuclei (the sublaterodorsal nucleus) drench spinal motor neurons in glycine and GABA—paralyzing every skeletal muscle in your body except the diaphragm and eye muscles.

· · ·

When the cerebral cortex wakes into conscious awareness a few seconds before the brainstem turns off that REM glycine lock, the sleeper experiences isolated sleep paralysis. Unable to lift a finger or speak while breathing only with the diaphragm, the awake brain projects residual REM dream figures directly into the open bedroom until the glycine brake releases.

· · ·

Taking a hot bath ninety minutes before bed helps you fall asleep faster not by warming you up, but by cooling your core down. Initiating slow-wave sleep requires your core brain temperature to drop by 1 degree Celsius. Warm water dilates vascular shunts in your palms and soles, turning your hands and feet into radiators that dump internal heat.

· · ·

During non-REM slow-wave sleep, the hippocampus replays the day's newly encoded spatial and episodic memories at ten to twenty times real-world speed inside 150-Hertz bursts called sharp-wave ripples. Locked to 12-Hertz thalamocortical sleep spindles, this nighttime replay transfers fragile short-term traces from the temporary hippocampal buffer into permanent neocortical storage.

· · ·

Luis de Lecea and Masashi Yanagisawa discovered in 1998 that the flip-flop switch between waking and sleeping is stabilized by just 70,000 hypothalamic neurons secreting the peptide orexin (hypocretin). Autoimmune destruction of those 70,000 cells causes Type 1 narcolepsy, where sudden laughter or surprise triggers instant REM muscle paralysis (cataplexy) in the middle of the day.

· · ·

Across mammals, total daily sleep scales inversely with body size and foraging exposure—from 19 hours a day in the little brown bat down to just 2 hours a day in wild African bush elephants. Tracked with actigraphy collars, wild matriarch elephants sleep standing up and frequently go forty-eight hours without REM sleep while leading herds across lion territory.
