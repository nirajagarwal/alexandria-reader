The Human Body · Chapter 10

Bone and Muscle

Piezoelectric Remodeling, Sliding Myosin Ratchets, and the Tendon Spring

Living bone is a composite material engineered on the exact same mechanical principle as steel-reinforced concrete. Flexible triple-helix collagen fibers provide tensile toughness against brittle shattering, while nanoscopic plates of hydroxyapatite mineral ($\text{Ca}_{10}(\text{PO}_4)_6(\text{OH})_2$) provide compressive stiffness, yielding a lightweight beam with tensile strength rivaling cast iron.

· · ·

Surgeon Julius Wolff observed in 1892 that spongy trabecular bone struts inside the human femur align with the exact mathematical lines of principal stress calculated by crane engineers. When impact squeezes fluid through microscopic bone canals past osteocytes, those cells sense the shear stress, inhibit sclerostin, and summon osteoblasts to thicken the loaded struts.

· · ·

Because bone remodeling requires continuous gravitational loading, astronauts floating in microgravity aboard the International Space Station suffer rapid skeletal loss. Without daily heel-strike impact, astronauts lose 1 to 1.5 percent of their hip and spine bone mineral density every single month while excreting the dissolved calcium through their kidneys.

· · ·

Every ten years, your skeleton is completely demolished and replaced. Multinucleated osteoclasts clamp onto old, micro-cracked bone surfaces like suction cups, pump out hydrochloric acid and cathepsin K to dissolve mineral and collagen pits, and let trailing osteoblasts lay down fresh lamellar bone behind them.

· · ·

Andrew Fielding Huxley and Hugh Huxley independently proved in 1954 that contracting muscles never shorten their constituent protein chains. Instead, thick myosin filaments walk along thin actin filaments using ATP-powered cross-bridge ratchets, sliding the two interdigitating arrays past one another to shorten the sarcomere by 30 percent.

· · ·

Rigor mortis locks a cadaver's joints stiff three hours after death because of a counterintuitive step in the myosin cycle. In a living muscle fiber, binding a fresh, unsplit molecule of ATP is what detaches the myosin head from actin after each power stroke; when ATP runs out after death, all cross-bridges remain permanently welded.

· · ·

When a weightlifter's tendon approaches rupture tension, the contracting muscle is shut down before the bone breaks by the Golgi tendon organ. Braided directly into the collagen tendon, this tension sensor fires inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord to silence motor neurons whenever mechanical force spikes dangerously.

· · ·

Human skeletal muscle fibers come in three metabolic gears. Slow-twitch Type I fibers packed with red myoglobin and mitochondria burn fatty acids for hours without fatiguing, fast-twitch Type IIa fibers blend oxidative and glycolytic power, and explosive Type IIx fibers split stored phosphocreatine and glycogen anaerobically for ten-second sprints.

· · ·

The Achilles tendon connecting your calf to your heel is not a passive rope; during running, it acts as an elastic strain spring with 93 percent hysteresis return. Stretching as the foot hits the pavement, it stores 35 joules of kinetic energy and snaps it back at toe-off, cutting the oxygen cost of running in half.

· · ·

Next-day muscle soreness after walking down a mountain (DOMS) has nothing to do with lactic acid, which clears from the blood within sixty minutes of exercise. Downhill walking forces contracting muscles to lengthen under load, mechanically popping weak sarcomeres and triggering an inflammatory repair cascade twenty-four hours later.

· · ·

The smallest motor units in the human body—aiming the extraocular eye muscles—connect a single motor neuron to just 5 muscle fibers for micrometer precision. By contrast, a single motor neuron in the quadriceps or calf gastrocnemius fires 2,000 muscle fibers at once for brute lift.

· · ·

Japanese biochemist Setsuro Ebashi discovered in 1963 the molecular switch of all movement. In resting muscle, long tropomyosin cables block the binding sites on actin until an electrical action potential releases calcium ions ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to bind troponin C and roll the cable out of the way.
