The Human Body · Chapter 1

The Heart

Wringing Muscle Bands, Silent Pacemakers, and Why Cardiac Cells Never Get Cancer

The left ventricle does not squeeze blood out like a closing fist. Spanish cardiologist Francisco Torrent-Guasp proved in 1973 that the myocardium is a single helical muscular band twisted like a Möbius strip. Contracting this band wrings blood out like a wet dishcloth, achieving a 60 percent ejection fraction from fibers that shorten by only 15 percent.

· · ·

Primary cancer of the heart muscle is almost non-existent in human medicine. Adult cardiomyocytes permanently exit the cell-division cycle shortly after birth, locking down the DNA replication machinery that tumors hijack. The heart trades away the ability to regenerate dead muscle after an infarction in exchange for near-total immunity to malignant mutation.

· · ·

The heart's coronary arteries are the only arteries in the human body that fill during muscular relaxation rather than contraction. During systole, the contracting left ventricle generates so much wall tension that it crushes its own internal vessels shut. Fresh oxygenated blood can rush into the heart muscle only during the brief diastolic pause between beats.

· · ·

A transplanted heart sitting inside a recipient's chest has zero vagus or sympathetic nerve connections to the brain. Yet it continues beating steadily at roughly 100 beats per minute on its own. Pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node leak sodium ions through hyperpolarization-activated $I_f$ channels (HCN4), running an autonomous electrical clock without brain supervision.

· · ·

The familiar lub-dub sound heard through René Laennec's stethoscope is not the sound of cardiac muscle contracting. Muscle fibers slide silently past one another. The two distinct thumps are hydraulic water-hammer slams produced when the mitral and tricuspid valves, followed by the aortic and pulmonary valves, snap shut against reverse pressure gradients.

· · ·

Roughly 7,000 liters of oxygenated blood rush through the heart's four chambers every twenty-four hours. Despite this torrent inside the rooms, the dense inner myocardium cannot absorb a single drop of oxygen directly through the chamber walls. If a three-millimeter coronary artery on the outer surface clogs, the starved muscle suffocates within minutes.

· · ·

Pathologist Adolfo de Bold discovered in 1979 that the heart is also an active endocrine gland. When excess blood volume stretches the thin walls of the upper atria, cardiac muscle cells secrete a hormone called Atrial Natriuretic Peptide. This chemical messenger travels to the kidneys and orders them to dump sodium and water into the urine.

· · ·

Before birth, the human fetal heart runs its left and right pumps in parallel rather than in series. Because the fetus's lungs are collapsed and filled with amniotic fluid, oxygenated blood from the placenta detours straight across an atrial trapdoor called the foramen ovale. The baby's first breath reverses chest pressure and slams that flap shut.

· · ·

An elite marathoner's heart and a hypertensive patient's failing heart both enlarge, but they grow in orthogonal directions. Endurance volume loading adds new contractile sarcomeres end-to-end in series, widening the inner chamber so it holds more blood. Chronic high blood pressure stacks sarcomeres side-by-side in parallel, thickening the wall until the chamber can barely fill.

· · ·

At rest, cardiac muscle extracts 70 to 80 percent of the oxygen from every milliliter of blood flowing through the coronary arteries. Skeletal muscles extract only 25 percent at rest, leaving a large reserve in the veins. Because the heart has almost zero extraction reserve left, it can survive a sprint only by dilating its coronary arteries fourfold.

· · ·

During open-heart bypass surgery, surgeons arrest a beating heart on command by flushing the coronary arteries with cold potassium chloride solution. Flooding the extracellular space with potassium flattens the normal electrical gradient across the cell membrane. That depolarization locks voltage-gated sodium channels in an inactivated state, holding the heart still until warm blood washes the potassium out.

· · ·

Across an eighty-year human lifespan, a 300-gram heart beats roughly three billion times without taking a single three-second pause. Each contraction performs about one joule of mechanical work to push blood against arterial resistance. Summed over eight decades, that fist-sized pump performs enough cumulative mechanical work to lift a thirty-ton railway car ten kilometers into the sky.
