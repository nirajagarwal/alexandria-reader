Twelve Plants · Chapter 12

The Oak and the Crooked Knee

Tannin Armor, Plugged Vessels, and the Two Thousand Trees Inside a Warship

Inspecting the royal dockyards at Chatham, Deptford, and Portsmouth in 1662, the Navy Commissioners of King Charles II confronted a material shortage that threatened England's command of the sea. A century of charcoal iron-smelting furnaces, glassworks, and unregulated felling during the English Civil War had stripped the southern counties of mature English oak (Quercus robur). The newly chartered Royal Society of London asked country scholar John Evelyn to investigate how the realm could replenish its naval timber. On October 15, 1662, Evelyn read his treatise Sylva, or A Discourse of Forest-Trees before the Society, turning woodland management into a science of state survival.

Launching a single 74-gun Royal Navy third-rate ship of the line—the backbone of eighteenth-century battle fleets—required felling roughly two thousand mature oak trees aged between one hundred and one hundred and fifty years. At thirty-five large trees to the acre, one warship swallowed nearly sixty acres of century-old forest, or roughly three thousand loads of squared timber weighing over two thousand tons. Shipwrights could not build battle hulls out of faster-growing pine, spruce, or beech because English oak combined three evolved anatomical and biochemical properties inside its heartwood:

  • Tylose-Plugged Watertight Vessels: In a living oak, only the outer twenty rings of pale sapwood carry water up from the roots. As older rings transition into dark inner heartwood, adjacent living parenchyma cells squeeze through microscopic border pits into the wide springwood xylem vessels and inflate like tiny airbags called tyloses. Whereas American red oak (Quercus rubra*) leaves its vessels open and leaks water along the grain, English white oak plugs its vessels completely with tyloses, making hull planks impervious to seawater seepage and blocking fungal threads from spreading down the timber.
  • Ellagitannin Antifungal Chemistry: To keep its dead central heartwood core from rotting away while the tree stands for four hundred years, Quercus robur impregnates its heartwood cell walls with up to ten percent dry weight of hydrolysable polyphenols called ellagitannins (castalagin and vescalagin). Those tannins bind and inactivate the cellulase enzymes secreted by wood-rotting fungi (Serpula lacrymans*), enabling oak keels and frames to survive for decades in damp, unventilated bilges.
  • * Continuous-Grain Compass and Knee Timber: A wooden warship rolling in Atlantic gales with seventy-four iron cannon slamming backward on its upper decks experienced immense racking forces at the right-angle joints where horizontal deck beams met vertical hull ribs. If a carpenter sawed an L-shaped bracket out of a straight tree trunk, the wood grain ran across the bend and sheared apart under cannon recoil. Dockyards therefore paid premium prices for compass timber and crooks—freestanding oaks grown in open pastures or hedgerows where low, heavy branches forked off the main trunk at ninety to one hundred and twenty degrees, providing natural knees with continuous, unbroken cellulose fibers running around the curve.

During the Napoleonic Wars, Vice-Admiral Cuthbert Collingwood assumed command of the British fleet at Trafalgar in October 1805 when Horatio Nelson fell. Collingwood grew so anxious over the Navy's dwindling supply of curved hedgerow oak that whenever he walked his country estate at Morpeth in Northumberland, he filled his coat pockets with acorns. He poked them into open banks and field corners whenever nobody was looking. Writing home to his wife Sarah in 1806, the admiral explained that he planted an oak wherever he found receptive soil so the Navy of England would never lack timber a century in the future. When ironclad hulls and steam engines replaced wooden warships in the 1860s, tens of thousands of oaks planted after Trafalgar were still only halfway to the century-old maturity required for a warship's hull.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: John Evelyn's 1662 Sylva presented to the Royal Society and Admiral Lord Collingwood planting acorns across Monmouthshire during the Napoleonic Wars to supply curved compass timber for 74-gun Royal Navy ships of the line.
