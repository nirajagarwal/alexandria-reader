Twelve Vaccines · Chapter 1

Smallpox and the Cowpox Cousin

How Milkmaid Blisters, Orphan Chains, and Split Needles Wiped Out a Human Plague

Across rural England in the 1700s, country dairymaids were famous for having clear, unscarred skin. At the time, smallpox killed one in three infected children and left survivors blinded or deeply pitted for life. Milkmaids regularly caught cowpox, a mild infection from blisters on cows' udders that left a few sores on their hands for a week. Once those sores healed, the milkmaids almost never caught smallpox.

In May 1796 in Berkeley, Gloucestershire, country doctor Edward Jenner put this farmyard observation to a test. He took a drop of fluid from a cowpox blister on the hand of milkmaid Sarah Nelmes. He scratched that fluid into the arm of a local boy named James Phipps. Phipps ran a mild fever for a single day and recovered. Six weeks later, Jenner scratched live smallpox pus into the boy's arm, and not a single blister formed.

Why did a mild cow virus protect a human against a killer plague? Smallpox and cowpox belong to the same family of poxviruses. Because they evolved from a shared ancestor, both viruses wear nearly identical protein coats on their outer surfaces. When cowpox multiplied harmlessly in the skin of Phipps's arm, his immune system built antibodies and memory cells shaped to grab those surface proteins. When real smallpox arrived six weeks later, it wore the same outer uniform. Phipps's trained antibodies swarmed and destroyed the virus before it could spread.

Getting that live virus across oceans before refrigerators existed required an extraordinary human relay. Dried cowpox threads often died on long sea voyages in tropical heat. In November 1803, Spanish physician Francisco Javier de Balmis sailed from A Coruña aboard the ship María Pita with twenty-two orphan boys between ages three and nine. None of the boys had ever caught cowpox or smallpox. At departure, Balmis scratched cowpox into the arms of two boys. Nine days later, just as their arm blisters ripened, he transferred fresh fluid into the next pair of boys. That living chain of blisters crossed the Atlantic and Pacific, bringing vaccination to hundreds of thousands of people across the Americas and Asia.

Yet smallpox still killed two million people a year in the 1960s until two simple tools finished the job. First, in 1965 at Wyeth Laboratories, microbiologist Benjamin Rubin ground down the eyelet of a sewing machine needle into a tiny two-pronged fork. Dipped into a vaccine vial, the slot between the two prongs held just 0.0025 milliliters of liquid by surface tension. That tiny drop was one-fourth the old syringe dose, stretching global vaccine supplies fourfold. Any village volunteer could learn in five minutes to dip the fork and jab a patient's arm fifteen times.

Second, epidemiologists William Foege and Donald Henderson realized they did not need to vaccinate every person on Earth at once. Instead, they used the mathematics of the herd immunity threshold $p_c$:

$$p_c = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}$$

In an unvaccinated town, each smallpox patient infected roughly $R_0 = 5$ other people. Once $p_c = 1 - 1/5 = 80\%$ of the people around an outbreak were immune, each sick person infected fewer than one new person, and the chain of transmission died out. World Health Organization teams tracked down every new smallpox patient, isolated them at home, and vaccinated a tight ring of family, neighbors, and coworkers around each village. Because smallpox could not hide in wild animals, that ring strategy cornered the last natural case on Earth—hospital cook Ali Maow Maalin in Merca, Somalia—in October 1977.
