Diphtheria, Tetanus, and the Disarmed Poison

How Gaston Ramon Used Formaldehyde and Kitchen Tapioca to Tame Two Bacterial Toxins

Most germs make you sick by multiplying across your organs, but the bacteria behind diphtheria and tetanus barely travel at all. Diphtheria bacteria camp inside a leathery gray patch at the back of a child's throat. Tetanus bacteria hide inside a deep, airless puncture wound from a rusty nail or garden thorn. From those tiny hideouts, both bacteria pump out a protein poison—a toxin—that enters the bloodstream. Diphtheria toxin shuts down protein factories inside heart and nerve cells. Tetanus toxin blocks the nerve switches that let muscles relax. Within days, it locks the jaw and chest in fatal spasms. Just one-millionth of a gram of tetanus toxin is enough to kill an adult human.

In 1890 in Berlin, Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato discovered that injecting animals with tiny drops of these bacterial broths caused their blood to fill with antitoxin antibodies. Giving horse antitoxin serum to a child choking with diphtheria saved thousands of lives. Yet there was a big catch. Borrowed horse antibodies were cleared from a child's blood in three weeks, leaving no lasting memory cells behind. Worse, a second injection of horse blood often triggered severe allergic shock. For permanent protection, human immune systems needed to practice on the poison itself without dying from it.

In 1923 at the Pasteur Institute annex in Garches outside Paris, veterinarian Gaston Ramon noticed something useful. A batch of diphtheria toxin treated with formalin—water mixed with formaldehyde to keep stray germs out—had lost its ability to kill guinea pigs. Ramon found that warming diphtheria or tetanus toxin with 0.3 to 0.4 percent formaldehyde at body temperature (37 degrees Celsius) for four weeks stitched tiny chemical bridges across the poison protein. Those bridges bolted the poison's cutting blade shut while leaving its outside bumps unchanged. Ramon called this disarmed poison a "toxoid." When injected into a child, the harmless toxoid trained antibodies that grabbed the real poison the instant it appeared.

While harvesting antitoxin from horses at Garches in 1925, Ramon noticed a second puzzle. Horses that developed a small, sterile lump of inflammation at the needle site made far more antibodies than horses whose skin stayed calm. A pure, clean protein often slips past the immune system unnoticed because it causes no tissue damage and rings no alarm bells. Ramon tested mixing his toxoid with breadcrumbs, olive oil, and ordinary kitchen tapioca starch from his pantry. Antibody levels jumped severalfold.

A year later in London, chemist Alexander Glenny achieved the same boost with a cleaner ingredient. He precipitated diphtheria toxoid onto tiny crystals of aluminum potassium sulfate, or alum. Those microscopic aluminum salts held the toxoid at the injection site like a slow-release sponge and irritated local muscle cells just enough to call immune scout cells to the spot.

Combined with killed whooping cough bacteria in the 1940s as the childhood DTP shot, diphtheria and tetanus toxoids transformed child health. Even better, when a pregnant mother receives a tetanus shot, her antibodies cross the placenta into her baby's blood. That borrowed shield protects newborns when umbilical cords are cut in unsterile conditions, cutting global newborn tetanus deaths from roughly 787,000 babies a year in 1988 to fewer than 25,000 a year within three decades.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In 1890 in Berlin, Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato discovered that diphtheria and tetanus bacteria kill not by spreading through every organ, but by releasing lethal protein poisons into the blood, and that horse serum containing antitoxin antibodies could save dying children for a few weeks. In 1923 at the Pasteur Institute annex in Garches outside Paris, veterinarian Gaston Ramon added a 0.3 to 0.4 percent splash of formalin (aqueous formaldehyde) to diphtheria toxin and warmed it at 37 degrees Celsius for a month, stitching chemical bridges across the poison so it lost all toxicity while keeping its outer shape as a harmless toxoid. In 1925 and 1926, Ramon added tapioca starch and Alexander Glenny in London added aluminum potassium sulfate (alum) to trigger a local alarm that multiplied antibody output.
Empirical Payoff: After routine childhood DTP immunization and maternal tetanus toxoid campaigns spread worldwide, neonatal tetanus deaths fell from roughly 787,000 babies per year in 1988 to fewer than 25,000 per year within three decades.
