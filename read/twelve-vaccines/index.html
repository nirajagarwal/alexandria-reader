Medicine & Immunology

Twelve Vaccines

How Cowpox Scabs, Weakened Viruses, Sugar Coats, and Fat Droplets Taught the Body to Fight Back

Catching a dangerous infection for the first time is a race against the clock. A virus or bacterium can double its numbers inside your body every hour. Meanwhile, your immune system takes a full week to find the right defensive cells, multiply them, and build custom antibodies that fit the invader's outer shell. If the germ destroys your lungs, liver, or nerves before that first week is up, you lose the race.

Once you survive an infection, however, a team of veteran memory cells stays behind in your lymph nodes and bone marrow for decades. The next time that same germ enters your body, those memory cells recognize its outer coat within hours. They flood your blood with antibodies before you even feel a fever.

Every vaccine is a way to give your immune system that first practice lesson without putting you through the deadly disease. Over the past two centuries, vaccine makers have invented twelve clever disguises to show your body the enemy's mugshot safely. They have borrowed mild animal cousins, dried viruses in glass flasks, and locked bacterial poisons shut with formaldehyde. They have walked jungle viruses through hundreds of eggs, brewed empty protein shells in baker's yeast, tied slippery bacterial sugars to proteins so babies can spot them, and pinned shape-shifting viral spikes inside microscopic fat bubbles.

Contents & Provenance Ledger (12 Chapters)
  • Introduction
    Teaching the Immune System With Harmless Mugshots Before the Real Fight
  • 1. Smallpox and the Cowpox Cousin
    How Milkmaid Blisters, Orphan Chains, and Split Needles Wiped Out a Human Plague
    In May 1796 in Berkeley, Gloucestershire, country physician Edward Jenner took matter from a cowpox blister on the hand of milkmaid Sarah Nelmes and scratched it into the arm of James Phipps, a boy of eight, then tested him six weeks later with live smallpox matter without a single pustule forming. In 1803, Spanish physician Francisco Javier de Balmis sailed from A Coruña with twenty-two orphan boys to carry live cowpox across the Atlantic by passing blister fluid from arm to arm every nine days. In 1965, Wyeth microbiologist Benjamin Rubin ground down the eye of a sewing machine needle into a two-pronged fork that held 0.0025 milliliters of vaccine by capillary tension, cutting the dose to one-fourth and enabling D. A. Henderson's World Health Organization teams to track down the last natural smallpox case in Merca, Somalia in October 1977.
  • 2. Rabies and the Dried Spinal Cord
    How Louis Pasteur Raced a Lethal Virus on Its Slow Climb Up the Nerves
    In July 1885 in Paris, young Joseph Meister arrived from Alsace at Louis Pasteur and Émile Roux's Rue d'Ulm laboratory with fourteen deep dog bites on his hands, legs, and thighs. Because rabies virus does not race through the bloodstream—it inches up motor nerves from the bite toward the spinal cord and brain at roughly one to two millimeters per hour—Pasteur had a two-to-four-week window to train the boy's immune system after the bite. Pasteur and Roux suspended rabid rabbit spinal cords inside glass flasks over potassium hydroxide crystals for up to fourteen days so dry air gradually killed the virus, then injected Meister with thirteen escalating doses over ten days from fourteen-day-dried cord to fresh one-day cord.
  • 3. Diphtheria, Tetanus, and the Disarmed Poison
    How Gaston Ramon Used Formaldehyde and Kitchen Tapioca to Tame Two Bacterial Toxins
    In 1890 in Berlin, Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato discovered that diphtheria and tetanus bacteria kill not by spreading through every organ, but by releasing lethal protein poisons into the blood, and that horse serum containing antitoxin antibodies could save dying children for a few weeks. In 1923 at the Pasteur Institute annex in Garches outside Paris, veterinarian Gaston Ramon added a 0.3 to 0.4 percent splash of formalin (aqueous formaldehyde) to diphtheria toxin and warmed it at 37 degrees Celsius for a month, stitching chemical bridges across the poison so it lost all toxicity while keeping its outer shape as a harmless toxoid. In 1925 and 1926, Ramon added tapioca starch and Alexander Glenny in London added aluminum potassium sulfate (alum) to trigger a local alarm that multiplied antibody output.
  • 4. Yellow Fever and the Hundredth Egg
    How Max Theiler Walked a Jungle Virus Through Mouse Brains and Chick Embryos
    In June 1927 in Lagos, Nigeria, Rockefeller Foundation researchers Adrian Stokes, Johannes Bauer, and N. Paul Hudson isolated yellow fever virus into rhesus monkeys from the blood of a Ghanaian patient named Asibi who survived a mild fever, though Stokes died of laboratory-contracted yellow fever two months later. Starting in 1930 at Harvard and the Rockefeller Institute in New York, Max Theiler and Hugh Smith passed the Asibi virus through mouse brains and then through hundreds of flasks of minced chick embryos whose brains and spinal cords had been surgically removed so the virus could not adapt to nerve tissue.
  • 5. Polio and the Killed-Versus-Live Rivalry
    How Jonas Salk's Formaldehyde Bath and Albert Sabin's Sugar Cube Stopped Summer Paralysis
    After John Enders, Thomas Weller, and Frederick Robbins proved at Boston Children's Hospital in 1949 that poliovirus could be grown in non-nervous tissue, Jonas Salk at the University of Pittsburgh grew all three poliovirus types in monkey kidney cells and soaked the filtered fluid in 1-to-4,000 formalin at 37 degrees Celsius for up to twelve days along a first-order exponential inactivation curve. Tested in 1.8 million schoolchildren by Thomas Francis Jr. in 1954 and announced on April 12, 1955, Salk's injected killed vaccine blocked poliovirus in the bloodstream from reaching motor nerves, while Albert Sabin at the University of Cincinnati bred weakened live strains given on a sugar cube by mouth starting in 1959–1961 to train gut immunity that stopped transmission altogether.
  • 6. Measles, Mumps, and the Memory Eraser
    How Maurice Hilleman Brewed a Chick-Embryo Trio That Protects Your Old Immune Memories
    In February 1954 in Boston, pediatricians Thomas Peebles and John Enders isolated measles virus from throat swabs and blood of schoolboy David Edmonston during an outbreak at the Fay School. At Merck in West Point, Pennsylvania, microbiologist Maurice Hilleman passed the Edmonston strain forty additional times through chick embryo cells at 32 degrees Celsius to create the gentler Moraten strain licensed in 1968, swabbed his own daughter Jeryl Lynn's throat in March 1963 to breed the mumps vaccine within four years, and combined them with Stanley Plotkin's rubella strain into the single MMR shot in 1971. In 2015 and 2019, Michael Mina, Rik de Swart, and Stephen Elledge proved why measles vaccination cut deaths from all other childhood infections in half.
  • 7. Hepatitis B and the Baker's Yeast Decoy
    From Baruch Blumberg's Blood Plasma Filter to the First Cancer-Preventing Vaccine Brewed in Vats
    In 1965 and 1968 at the Fox Chase Cancer Center in Philadelphia, Baruch Blumberg and Werner London discovered that the blood of chronic hepatitis B carriers swarmed with non-infectious 22-nanometer spherical protein shells—empty decoys made of the virus's outer surface protein without any viral DNA inside. After Maurice Hilleman at Merck purified and triple-inactivated those spheres from human plasma in 1981, William Rutter at UCSF, Pablo Valenzuela at Chiron, and Benjamin Hall at the University of Washington spliced the single gene for that surface protein into ordinary baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) in 1981, winning FDA approval in July 1986 for the first genetically engineered human vaccine.
  • 8. Hib and the Sugar-Coat Trick
    How John Robbins and Rachel Schneerson Tied a Slippery Bacterial Coat to a Protein So Babies Could See It
    Before 1985, Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) struck roughly 20,000 American children under age five every year—causing bacterial meningitis, deafness, and throat-closing epiglottitis—yet pure vaccines made from the bacterium's outer polyribosylribitol phosphate sugar coat failed completely in infants under eighteen months. Building on Oswald Avery and Walther Goebel's 1929 Rockefeller Institute finding that linking a bacterial sugar to a protein made it visible to rabbit immune systems, John Robbins and Rachel Schneerson at the National Institutes of Health in Bethesda in 1980 chemically tied Hib's sugar coat to a harmless carrier protein such as tetanus or diphtheria toxoid.
  • 9. HPV and the Hollow Virus Shell
    How Jian Zhou and Ian Frazer Built Self-Assembling Empty Shells That Stop Cervical Cancer
    After Harald zur Hausen at the German Cancer Research Center in Heidelberg isolated human papillomavirus types 16 and 18 from cervical tumors in 1983 and 1984, vaccine makers faced a dead end because HPV refused to multiply in laboratory cell cultures and carried cancer-causing genes. In 1991 at the University of Queensland in Brisbane, Jian Zhou, Xiao-Yi Sun, and Ian Frazer—followed independently by Douglas Lowy and John Schiller at the U.S. National Cancer Institute—expressed the virus's single L1 outer shell gene in cells and discovered that 360 copies of L1 spontaneously snapped together into hollow 55-nanometer virus-like particles with zero viral DNA.
  • 10. Rotavirus and the Cow-Human Swap
    How Paul Offit, Fred Clark, and Stanley Plotkin Shuffled Eleven RNA Strands to Save Infants From Dehydration
    In 1973 at the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Ruth Bishop, Geoffrey Davidson, Ian Holmes, and Brian Ruck used electron microscopy on duodenal biopsies to identify wheel-shaped rotaviruses as the leading cause of severe infant dehydration worldwide. Starting in 1980 at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and the Wistar Institute, Fred Clark, Paul Offit, and Stanley Plotkin isolated a calf rotavirus strain (WC3) from a Pennsylvania dairy farm and took advantage of rotavirus's segmented genome—eleven separate strands of double-stranded RNA—by co-infecting cells with calf and human strains to breed five hybrid viruses carrying ten cow genes for safety and one human surface gene for immunity.
  • 11. Ebola and the Trojan Horse Ring
    How Heinz Feldmann Dressed a Harmless Livestock Virus in Ebola's Coat to Stop an Outbreak in Guinea
    At the Public Health Agency of Canada's National Microbiology Laboratory in Winnipeg between 2001 and 2005, Heinz Feldmann, Ute Ströher, and Steven Jones engineered a harmless Trojan horse by taking Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV)—a livestock virus that causes mild mouth sores in cattle—snipping out its native surface gene, and inserting the single outer glycoprotein gene of Zaire Ebola virus. During the 2015 West African epidemic in coastal Guinea, World Health Organization teams led by Ana Maria Henao-Restrepo adapted the smallpox ring-vaccination strategy in the Ebola Ça Suffit! trial, tracing 90 clusters of 7,651 contacts and contacts-of-contacts of newly diagnosed patients.
  • 12. Prefusion Spike and the Fat Bubble
    How Barney Graham, Jason McLellan, and Pieter Cullis Pinned a Shape-Shifting Protein Inside a Lipid Nanoparticle
    In 1966 in Washington, D.C., a formalin-killed vaccine against Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) failed catastrophically—hospitalizing 80 percent of infected vaccinees—because formalin caused the virus's surface fusion protein to snap from its live compact 'prefusion' shape into a spent rod before the immune system ever saw it. At the NIH Vaccine Research Center and Dartmouth between 2013 and 2017, Barney Graham and Jason McLellan solved this structural trap for RSV and coronaviruses by substituting two rigid proline amino acids (2P) at the hinge of the spike protein to pin it in its live prefusion shape, then packaged that genetic recipe inside Pieter Cullis's four-lipid nanoparticles.
