Attention · Chapter 10

Variable-Ratio Salience and the Gain Knob

How Unpredictable Rewards and Brainstem Norepinephrine Lock the Gaze

A hungry pigeon inside an acrylic conditioning box at Harvard University in 1957 struck an illuminated plastic disk with its beak up to five times per second for fourteen straight hours without pausing. When an automated grain hopper delivered food on a predictable fixed schedule—say, after every twentieth peck—the pigeon worked in bursts and rested after each meal. When B. F. Skinner and Charles Ferster wired the hopper to a variable-ratio schedule that paid out on an unpredictable average of once every hundred pecks, the bird's rest pauses disappeared. Its attention welded permanently to the disk.

In Schedules of Reinforcement, Skinner and Ferster documented that variable-ratio schedules produce a sustained high rate of responding and make the conditioned behavior remarkably resistant to extinction across animal species. Under a fixed schedule, three or four unrewarded actions immediately signal that the machine is broken, allowing attention to disengage and look elsewhere. Under a variable-ratio schedule, however, a long dry spell is a normal part of the statistical pattern. Every unrewarded check leaves open the possibility that the very next check will pay off. The occasional surprise jackpot then triggers a massive midbrain prediction-error spike that welds executive focus back onto the lever or scrolling screen.

Thirty-six years later, in 1993, the neuroscientists Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson at the University of Michigan proved that this compulsive attentional capture is driven by a brain circuit completely separate from pleasure. By selectively lesioning or stimulating mesolimbic dopamine pathways in the nucleus accumbens, they dissociated hedonic enjoyment ("liking"), which relies on tiny opioid hotspots, from incentive salience ("wanting"). Dopamine does not create subjective satisfaction. Instead, it acts as an attentional magnet that commands the visual and motor systems to orient toward, approach, and check a cue even when the person actively reports disliking the outcome.

While midbrain dopamine tags external cues with magnetic salience, a tiny cluster of roughly thirty thousand blue-pigmented neurons in the brainstem—the locus coeruleus—controls whether the cerebral cortex stays locked on its current task or breaks away to scan the room. Those brainstem axons branch across the entire neocortex to adjust the signal-to-noise ratio of sensory neurons. In 2005, Gary Aston-Jones and Jonathan Cohen formulated Adaptive Gain Theory. They showed that norepinephrine released by the locus coeruleus switches cortical circuits across three distinct operating modes.

  • * Phasic exploitation mode: Moderate baseline norepinephrine paired with sharp, target-locked bursts amplifies neural gain specifically during task-relevant inputs, locking the mind cleanly onto its chosen activity.
  • * High-tonic exploration mode: When reward utility drops or environmental uncertainty spikes, the locus coeruleus shifts into continuous high baseline firing, flooding the cortex with norepinephrine so that every background stimulus triggers a response and the animal scans restlessly for alternatives.
  • * Low-tonic disengagement: When baseline firing drops toward zero, cortical gain collapses into drowsiness and mind-wandering.

Because the locus coeruleus also controls the sympathetic dilator muscle of the iris, its firing rate is visible directly on the outside of the eye. In Gary Aston-Jones and Jonathan Cohen's primate recordings, a monkey's baseline pupil diameter—governed directly by tonic firing in the thirty thousand neurons of the locus coeruleus—tracked its internal gain state continuously. That pupil measurement predicted whether the animal would stay locked on its current visual target or abandon the task to explore the room several seconds before its hand moved.
