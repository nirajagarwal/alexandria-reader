Attention · Chapter 4

Biased Competition and Neural Gain

How Two Objects Fight for One Receptive Field Inside the Visual Cortex

A tungsten microelectrode lowered into area V4 of a rhesus macaque's visual cortex in 1985 recorded a striking electrical tug-of-war. At the National Institute of Mental Health in Bethesda, Jeffrey Moran and Robert Desimone isolated a single V4 neuron and mapped the patch of visual space it monitored. Whenever a vertical red bar appeared inside that receptive field, the cell fired a brisk storm of action potentials. Whenever a horizontal green bar appeared in the same patch alone, the cell barely ticked. Then Moran and Desimone displayed both the red bar and the green bar inside that single neuron's receptive field at the exact same time. They trained the monkey to keep its eyes locked on a central dot while shifting its covert attention to one bar or the other.

This experiment exposed a fundamental architectural dilemma of the primate visual system. In the primary visual cortex (area V1) at the back of the skull, each neuron monitors a tiny pinpoint of space covering less than half a degree of visual angle. As signals travel forward along the ventral object-recognition pathway into area V4 and the inferior temporal (IT) cortex, receptive fields widen to span ten to twenty degrees. That expansion allows the brain to recognize a face or a coffee cup regardless of where it sits on a table. However, wide receptive fields create a severe crowding crisis whenever two objects sit side by side.

When both the red bar and the green bar fall inside the same V4 receptive field without top-down guidance, the two inputs mutually inhibit each other. The green distractor pulls the neuron's firing rate down into a muddled average, preventing downstream areas from telling what is on the screen. To explain how the brain resolves this collision, Robert Desimone and the Cambridge psychologist John Duncan formulated the Biased Competition model in 1995. They demonstrated that objects in the visual field actively compete for representation, analysis, and control across the visual cortex, while top-down feedback biases that contest toward behaviorally relevant information.

In the Desimone-Duncan circuit, neighboring cortical columns tuned to different colors and shapes are linked through local inhibitory interneurons. When the monkey is instructed to attend to the red bar, top-down feedback from the prefrontal cortex and frontal eye fields sends a modest excitatory current to the red-tuned input synapses. Those boosted synapses drive the local inhibitory interneurons harder, clamping down on the neighboring green-bar synapses. As a result, the V4 neuron's wide receptive field shrinks like a drawstring bag around the attended target alone. It acts as if the competing object had been erased from the screen.

In 2001, Pascal Fries working in Desimone's laboratory uncovered a second amplifier that makes this competition even more efficient. Rather than simply forcing cortical cells to burn huge amounts of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) firing more spikes, attention locks the attended V4 neurons into synchronized 35-to-70-hertz gamma oscillations. Their action potentials arrive at downstream inferior temporal neurons within the same three-millisecond window. That tight timing triggers downstream firing while unsynchronized distractor spikes fizzle out. In Moran and Desimone's recordings, both the preferred red bar and the non-preferred green bar sat inside a single macaque V4 neuron's receptive field. Shifting the monkey's covert attention from the green bar to the red bar caused that exact cell's firing rate to jump nearly threefold without either physical bar moving a millimeter.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: Jeffrey Moran and Robert Desimone's 1985 NIH microelectrode recordings in macaque area V4 and Robert Desimone and John Duncan's 1995 formulation of the Biased Competition model of selective visual attention.
Empirical Payoff: When Moran and Desimone placed both a preferred red bar and a non-preferred green bar inside a single macaque V4 neuron's receptive field, shifting the monkey's attention from the green bar to the red bar caused the exact same cell's firing rate to jump nearly threefold without either physical bar moving a millimeter on the screen.
