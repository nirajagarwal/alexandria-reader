Attention · Chapter 5

The Mismatch Alarm

Pavlov's Interrupted Dogs, Silent Tones, and the Mathematics of Surprise

Whenever an unfamiliar visitor or medical inspector stepped into Ivan Pavlov's soundproof dog laboratories in St. Petersburg around 1910, months of delicate conditioned-reflex training evaporated on the spot. A dog that had been conditioned across hundreds of trials to salivate at the exact click of a metronome or the flash of an electric bulb immediately stopped secreting saliva. It froze its posture, pricked up its ears, dilated its pupils, and rotated its head toward the stranger's boots. Pavlov called this involuntary biological override the orienting or what-is-it reflex. He recognized that nature could never afford to give an animal unbreakable concentration, because any creature whose top-down focus was so airtight that a snapping twig failed to interrupt its meal would be killed and eaten before nightfall.

For half a century after Pavlov, sensory physiologists assumed that this bottom-up circuit breaker was tripped purely by raw physical intensity. A louder crash or a brighter flash was thought to mechanically overload sensory relays in the brainstem's superior colliculus and yank the eyes toward the disturbance. That simple energy model collapsed in 1960 at Moscow State University, when the Soviet neurophysiologist Evgeny Sokolov placed human volunteers inside a darkened, soundproof chamber. He played a uniform sixty-decibel acoustic tone repeatedly at fixed intervals while recording palmar skin conductance, finger blood-vessel constriction, and occipital alpha brainwaves.

On the first three presentations of the sixty-decibel tone, every volunteer exhibited a classic orienting response: skin conductance spiked, peripheral blood vessels constricted, and the resting alpha rhythm vanished as the cortex snapped to alert. By the fifteenth or twentieth repetition of the exact same tone, the autonomic alarm disappeared completely as the subject habituated to the noise. Then, on the twenty-first trial, Sokolov altered the stimulus not by blasting a louder sound, but by dropping the tone to a quiet forty decibels or shortening its duration by a fraction of a second.

Instantly, the volunteer's blood vessels constricted and their skin conductance spiked with the same intensity as when the loud tone had first sounded. Even when Sokolov muted the twenty-first tone altogether in total silence, the subject's autonomic nervous system fired a sharp orienting alarm at the exact millisecond the missing sound was scheduled to arrive. Sokolov had proved that the cerebral cortex and hippocampus continuously construct an internal neuronal model of the environment's statistical regularities. Involuntary attention is triggered not by raw sensory wattage, but by a mismatch between incoming reality and the brain's active prediction.

In 2005, computational neuroscientists Laurent Itti at the University of Southern California and Pierre Baldi at the University of California, Irvine, formalized Sokolov's mismatch detector using exact Bayesian information theory. As an observer inspects a scene, their visual cortex maintains a prior probability distribution $P(\mathcal{M})$ over a space of internal hypotheses $\mathcal{M}$ describing the world. When a new sensory observation $D$ arrives, the brain updates that prior via Bayes' theorem into a posterior distribution $P(\mathcal{M}\mid D)$. The bottom-up pull exerted on attention—which Itti and Baldi termed Bayesian surprise $S(D, \mathcal{M})$—equals the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the prior and posterior distributions:

$$S(D, \mathcal{M}) = D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(P(\mathcal{M}\mid D)\parallel P(\mathcal{M})\bigr) = \int_{\mathcal{M}} P(\mathcal{M}\mid D) \log_2 \frac{P(\mathcal{M}\mid D)}{P(\mathcal{M})}\,d\mathcal{M}$$

Under this mathematical law, a high-contrast brick wall or the roar of an aircraft cabin carries massive physical energy but produces zero divergence between prior and posterior beliefs, so the orienting system ignores it. Tracking human eye movements across thousands of natural video frames, Itti and Baldi demonstrated that seventy-two percent of spontaneous human gaze shifts land on image patches that maximize this exact Kullback-Leibler divergence. In Sokolov's Moscow chamber, repeating a sixty-decibel tone twenty times extinguished the subject's orienting response completely. Yet dropping the twenty-first tone to forty decibels—or omitting it on schedule—triggered an immediate skin-conductance spike because the quiet violated the cortex's timing template.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: After Ivan Pavlov observed around 1910 in St. Petersburg that a stranger's footsteps instantly froze conditioned salivation via the orienting reflex, Evgeny Sokolov in 1960 at Moscow State University proved that a quieter or omitted tone triggers the same autonomic alarm, formalized in 2005 by Laurent Itti and Pierre Baldi as Kullback-Leibler Bayesian surprise.
Empirical Payoff: In Sokolov's Moscow chamber, repeating a sixty-decibel tone twenty times extinguished the subject's orienting response completely, yet dropping the twenty-first tone to forty decibels—or omitting it on schedule—triggered an immediate skin-conductance spike because the quiet violated the cortex's timing template.
