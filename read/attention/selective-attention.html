Attention · Chapter 1

Selective Attention

Why a Ten-Million-Bit Sensory Flood Passes Through a Fifty-Bit Conscious Gate

Ten million bits of raw optical data stream down the two million axons of the human optic nerves every second. Simultaneously, the skin, muscles, and inner ears send another million bits of tactile and acoustic traffic toward the brain. Yet conscious awareness can decode only forty to fifty bits per second. If the cerebral cortex attempted to compute the meaning of every phoneme, shadow, and shoe pressure at once, its metabolic circuits would lock up. Selective attention is the biological allocation mechanism that solves this bandwidth crisis. It routes a tiny fraction of sensory traffic into high-resolution cortical processing while actively suppressing the rest.

In 1953, the British telecommunications engineer Colin Cherry walked into his acoustics laboratory at Imperial College London to test how humans survive sensory overload. He called this everyday mystery the cocktail party problem, asking how a listener recognizes what one speaker is saying when several other people are talking at the exact same time. Cherry built a dichotic listening rig using magnetic tape to isolate the physical variables at work. He fed two different spoken prose passages into a listener's left and right headphones simultaneously. He then instructed the subject to repeat—or shadow—every word arriving in the left ear alone while ignoring the right ear.

His listeners tracked the assigned voice with near-perfect accuracy. They spoke out loud in a flat, monotonous rhythm just fractions of a second behind the moving tape. Yet when Cherry stopped the machine and asked what had happened in the right ear, their memory was almost completely blank. Listeners noticed if the unattended voice switched to a 400-hertz pure tone or changed from a man to a woman. However, they never noticed when the unattended speech switched from English to German, or even when the tape was played backward.

Five years later, in 1958, Donald Broadbent at the Applied Psychology Research Unit in Cambridge studied Royal Navy air-traffic controllers. Controllers sitting in noisy towers made dangerous errors whenever two pilots called in over a single shared loudspeaker at the same moment. Broadbent proposed that all incoming sensory wires feed a parallel short-term buffer before hitting a physical Y-shaped filter. Only signals matching a chosen physical feature—such as spatial location or vocal pitch—are allowed past the gate. Accepted signals then enter the single limited-capacity decision channel. Inside that conscious channel, the maximum information transmission rate $R$ in bits per second across $N$ equiprobable choices at bandwidth $B$ obeys the Hick-Hyman law.

$$R = B \log_2(N + 1)$$

Identifying a single spoken word from a working vocabulary requires roughly ten to twelve bits of decision capacity. Consequently, a fifty-bit channel can parse only four or five words per second. That rate matches the speed of a single human speaker and leaves zero spare room for a second conversation. Still, Broadbent's rigid on-off switch left an everyday anomaly unexplained. Why does a guest shadowing a conversation at a loud party immediately hear their own name spoken softly across the room?

At Oxford University in 1960, the doctoral researcher Anne Treisman discovered that the brain uses an attenuator rather than a hard switch. Unattended sensory channels are turned down like a dimmer knob, reducing their signal volume without cutting the wire completely. Damped words still trickle upward into an internal mental dictionary. Inside that dictionary, biologically urgent entries—such as one's own name or a shout of fire—keep permanently lowered firing thresholds. Even weak acoustic energy is enough to trip those primed entries and hijack conscious focus. When Anne Treisman swapped the two prose narratives mid-sentence between her Oxford subjects' left and right headphones in 1960, listeners unconsciously jumped to the ignored ear for one or two words. They followed the meaning just long enough to keep the sentence grammatical before snapping back to the assigned channel.
