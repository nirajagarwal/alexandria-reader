Attention · Chapter 6

Spotlight Focus Versus Open Vigilance

How the Left and Right Hemispheres Divide Narrow Grasping from Broad Watchfulness

Inside a neurology ward at the University of Milan in 1978, Edoardo Bisiach and Claudio Luzzatti sat beside two patients who had suffered right-hemisphere parietal strokes and asked them to close their eyes. Neither patient was blind, and both had lived in Milan for decades. The doctors asked each patient to imagine standing on the north steps of the Piazza del Duomo, facing the great cathedral from the opposite side of the square, and to name every building visible in their mind's eye. Both patients fluently described the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele and the arcades along the right side of the square. However, they omitted every single palace and shop standing on the left.

Moments later, Bisiach and Luzzatti asked the exact same patients to imagine walking across the cobblestones, climbing the cathedral steps, and turning around to face the square from the opposite direction. The neurologists reported in Cortex that both patients exhibited the exact same unilateral neglect when describing a single familiar piazza from two opposite mental viewpoints in rapid succession. Every landmark the patients had just described on their right now vanished because it sat on their imagined left. Meanwhile, the buildings they had completely forgotten sixty seconds earlier reappeared in crisp detail.

This clinical dissociation reveals why the vertebrate brain does not have a single unified attention system. Instead, evolution split attention across the two cerebral hemispheres to solve two incompatible biological tasks at the same time. An animal feeding on the ground must lock a narrow, high-resolution spotlight onto a tiny seed to grasp it accurately. Yet it must simultaneously keep a wide-angle, uncentered watch across the sky for hawks. In 1979, the neurobiologist Lesley Rogers discovered that domestic chicks and songbirds divide these two attentional modes strictly between their left and right hemispheres.

  • * Left-hemisphere spotlight focus: Wired to the right eye and right hand, the left hemisphere specializes in narrow, foveal, category-based grasping built to pick seeds from gravel, manipulate tools, and execute step-by-step routines.
  • * Right-hemisphere open vigilance: Wired initially to the left eye, the right hemisphere sustains broad, panoramic alertness across the entire environment, monitoring for predators, novel faces, and unexpected spatial shifts.
  • * Asymmetric spatial coverage (Mesulam, 1981): As the neurologist Marek Marsel Mesulam demonstrated, the human right parietal cortex monitors both the left and right halves of space, whereas the left parietal cortex monitors only the right half of space.

Because of Mesulam's asymmetry, a stroke in the left parietal lobe rarely causes permanent spatial neglect, since the intact right hemisphere still watches both sides of the room. When a stroke destroys the right parietal lobe, however, panoramic vigilance collapses and the left half of space loses its only monitor. Left unopposed, the intact left hemisphere locks its narrow spotlight stubbornly onto the right edge of whatever is in front of it. Patients with right-parietal hemispatial neglect shave only the right side of their faces, eat food only from the right half of their plates, and draw clock faces with all twelve numbers crammed into the right semicircle. When Bisiach and Luzzatti asked patient I. G. to imagine walking across the Piazza del Duomo and turning around to face the cathedral doors, the northern palaces shifted from his imagined left to his imagined right. Those buildings—completely invisible to his mind's eye sixty seconds earlier—instantly reappeared in vivid detail.
