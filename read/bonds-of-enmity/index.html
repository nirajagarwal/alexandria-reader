Conflict & Game Theory

Bonds of Enmity

How Blood Feuds, Rivalries, and Escalation Traps Lock Adversaries Together

Anger is a brief physiological reflex. A sudden insult or boundary violation triggers a surge of catecholamines from the adrenal medulla, elevating heart rate and priming skeletal muscle for immediate confrontation. Left to its own metabolic half-life, that neurochemical spike decays within minutes. Enmity is something altogether different. It is not a hot emotion but a cold, durable architecture—a coupled control system in which two adversaries become structurally locked into each other's decision loops across years, generations, or centuries.

Standard economic theory assumes that rational agents seek to maximize their own absolute payoffs, treating the welfare of competitors with indifference. Inside an architecture of enmity, that independence dissolves. The utility function of Actor A becomes negatively coupled to the utility of Actor B, until inflicting a relative loss on the adversary generates a higher marginal reward than securing a private gain. Once this coupling takes hold, ordinary feedback mechanisms invert. A defensive wall built solely to protect a city's grain supply registers on the opponent's ledger as preparation for an unpunishable first strike. A shared language or near-identical creed ceases to be a bridge of solidarity and becomes a threat to group distinctiveness that must be policed with lethal violence.

Because these systems are self-reinforcing, goodwill and pleasant social contact almost never dismantle them. An enmity loop is a mechanical equilibrium maintained by asymmetric information, indivisibility of status, credible retaliation protocols, and the absence of an external guarantor. Understanding how rivals become fused together—and why breaking the loop requires institutional surgery rather than moral persuasion—demands examining the exact blueprints of the machine.
