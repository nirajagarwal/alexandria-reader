Bonds of Enmity · Chapter 4

The Priority Trap

Isaac Newton, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and the Calculus War

Bound in quarto calfskin and distributed to the academies of Europe in April 1712, the Commercium Epistolicum D. Johannis Collins, et aliorum de Analysi Promota presented itself as a model of judicial impartiality. Two months earlier, the Royal Society of London had appointed an eleven-member committee of mathematicians, astronomers, and foreign ambassadors to audit the surviving letters and manuscripts of its former mathematical correspondent John Collins. Their charge was to settle the bitter twenty-year question of whether the German polymath Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz had invented the differential and integral calculus independently, or whether he had stolen the method of fluxions from Sir Isaac Newton.

The committee's Latin verdict was categorical: Newton was the first inventor of the calculus by more than a decade, and Leibniz had seen Newton's foundational letters during a visit to London in October 1676 before publishing his own system without attribution. An anonymous twenty-page review of the report in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society praised the commissioners as a numerous body of gentlemen from different nations whose integrity could not be questioned. Neither the readers in Paris and Berlin nor Leibniz himself knew the forensic reality buried in the Royal Society's archives: Isaac Newton, serving as President of the Royal Society, had personally packed the committee with his own disciples, drafted the entire Commercium Epistolicum report in his own hand, and written the anonymous glowing review of his own verdict.

Why did two of the greatest intellects of the Scientific Revolution spend the final decades of their lives exchanging forged pseudonyms, stacked tribunals, and mathematical ambushes? As the sociologist of science Robert K. Merton demonstrated, intellectual priority disputes turn far more venomous than commercial rivalries because of the reward architecture of science. In material commerce, property is divisible: two wool merchants or railway barons can split a territory sixty-forty and both grow rich. In fundamental discovery, property rights are strictly indivisible and tied to eponymy and immortality. Being the second person to discover the law of universal gravitation or the fundamental theorem of calculus carries a marginal prestige payoff near zero.

The historical record reconstructed from both men's private notebooks shows how an indivisible prize combined with secretive paranoia to spring the priority trap:

  1. 1. The Unpublished Vault (1665–1669): While retreating from the Great Plague to Woolsthorpe Manor in 1665 and 1666, young Isaac Newton developed his method of fluxions, treating mathematical curves as the paths of moving points with instantaneous velocities ($\dot{x}$, $\dot{y}$). Terrified of public criticism and priority theft, Newton refused to put his treatise De Analysi into print, circulating it only to a handful of Cambridge colleagues and hiding his methods inside Latin letter-count anagrams.
  2. 2. The Independent Breakthrough (1675–1684): Working in Paris in autumn 1675 without access to Newton's fluxional papers, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz arrived at the calculus from the opposite geometric direction—summing infinitesimal differences—and invented the operational symbols $\int y\,dx$ and $dy/dx$. In October 1684, Leibniz published his six-page paper Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis in the Leipzig journal Acta Eruditorum, giving Europe its first printed rules of differentiation.
  3. 3. The Escalation Relay (1696–1708): For twelve years, the two men maintained tense courtesy until partisans on both sides turned priority into national honor. In 1696, Leibniz's ally Johann Bernoulli circulated the Brachistochrone problem—finding the curve of fastest descent under gravity—explicitly designed to humble Newton (to test the pulse of the English analysts, as he put it); Newton received the problem at four o'clock in the afternoon after a full day running the Royal Mint, solved it before going to bed, and mailed it back anonymously, prompting Bernoulli's famous remark that he recognized the lion by his claw (tanquam ex ungue leonem). In 1708, Newton's Oxford lieutenant John Keill formally accused Leibniz of plagiarism in print, locking both camps into total war until Leibniz died isolated in Hanover in November 1716 with only his secretary attending his funeral.

The irony of Newton's total victory inside Britain was the structural penalty it imposed on his own followers. Newton's fluxional dot-notation ($\dot{x}$) was cumbersome for multivariable problems and higher-order derivatives, whereas Leibniz's differential operator ($d/dx$) functioned as an algebraic machine that guided the hand through partial differential equations and variational mechanics. By boycotting Leibniz's continental differential notation out of loyalty to Newton's dot-notation fluxions, British mathematicians crippled their own analytical progress for a full century until Cambridge students Charles Babbage, John Herschel, and George Peacock rebelled in 1812.
