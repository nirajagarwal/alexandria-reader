Biography & Computation

Alan Turing

Computability, Codebreaking, and the Patterns of Living Form

Before the middle of the twentieth century, science treated a mathematical proof, an industrial machine, and a growing biological embryo as three unrelated domains. Logicians viewed deduction as an exercise of pure human intuition. Engineers built special-purpose calculators where every mathematical operation was frozen into brass gears or soldered relays. Biologists struggled to explain how a symmetrical ball of cells could organize itself into dappled skin or spiral leaves without invoking an immaterial life force.

Alan Mathison Turing dissolved the boundaries separating those three worlds by asking a single question across all of them. How do simple, local mechanical rules generate complex global order? Born in London in 1912 and educated at Sherborne School and King's College, Cambridge, Turing refused to separate pure theory from physical hardware. When confronted with a foundational question in mathematical logic, he imagined a clerk writing symbols on an endless strip of paper tape. When confronted with encrypted military radio traffic or the round-off errors of matrix algebra, he designed electromagnetic relay circuits, acoustic mercury tubes, and Bayesian scoring ledgers.

What united Turing's work was his habit of stripping every problem down to its bare discrete or differential transitions. By encoding the rules of a machine as ordinary numbers on a tape, he proved that a single universal device can imitate any other machine. At the same time, he proved that certain mathematical questions lie forever beyond mechanical decision. In his hands, logic became physical machinery, codebreaking became sequential statistical inference, and embryology became the mathematics of coupled chemical waves.

Contents & Provenance Ledger (12 Chapters)
  • Introduction
    The Mechanist of Logic, Secrecy, and Living Form
  • 1. The Sherborne Notebook and the Nature of Spirit
    How Cycling Sixty Miles to School and Losing Christopher Morcom Shaped a Materialist Mind
    Alan Mathison Turing cycling sixty miles from Southampton to Sherborne School in Dorset during the May 1926 General Strike, forming an intense scientific friendship with fellow student Christopher Collan Morcom until Morcom's death from bovine tuberculosis in February 1930, and writing his 1932 manuscript 'Nature of Spirit' for Sara Morcom examining Arthur Eddington's quantum indeterminacy and brain states.
  • 2. The Central Limit Theorem Rediscovered Alone
    King's College Fellowship and the Probabilistic Bridge to Mathematical Foundations
    Turing at King's College, Cambridge in 1933–1935, independently proving the Lindeberg-Feller Central Limit Theorem in his fellowship dissertation 'On the Gaussian Error Function' without knowing Jarl Waldemar Lindeberg had proved it in 1922, winning election as a Fellow of King's College at twenty-two in March 1935, and attending Max Newman's spring 1935 Part III lectures on Kurt Gödel and David Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem.
  • 3. The Paper Tape and the Uncomputable Number
    How a Mechanical Clerk Destroyed David Hilbert's Dream of Complete Decidability
    Turing's 1936 paper 'On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem' in the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, defining a-machines (Turing machines) with an infinite one-dimensional paper tape, finite state tables, integer description numbers D(M), the Universal Turing Machine U, and Cantor diagonalization to prove the printing and halting problems undecidable.
  • 4. The Princeton Ordinal Hierarchies
    Alonzo Church's Lambda Calculus, Emil Post, and Oracle Machines Over the Abyss
    Turing at Princeton University from 1936 to 1938 working with Alonzo Church, proving the formal equivalence of lambda-definability and Turing computability, and completing his May 1938 Princeton PhD dissertation 'Systems of Logic Based on Ordinals' introducing transfinite ordinal logics and the O-machine (oracle machine) for relative computability.
  • 5. The Riemann Zeta Gear-Wheel Machine
    Cutting Brass Cams in Cambridge to Hunt Zeroes on the Critical Line
    Turing in 1938–1939 securing a forty-pound Royal Society grant to machine a thirty-wheel bronze analog mechanical computer at Cambridge (modeled on Lord Kelvin's Liverpool tide-predicting machine) to evaluate the Riemann zeta function on the critical line, leaving the gears unpacked in September 1939, and later running his 'Turing method' on the Manchester Mark 1 in June 1950 to verify the first 1,104 non-trivial zeroes.
  • 6. The Electromechanical Bombe and the Diagonal Board
    Turning Enigma's Self-Reciprocal Flaw Into a Parallel Contradiction Engine
    Turing at Bletchley Park's Hut 8 in 1939–1940 extending Marian Rejewski's 1938 Polish Bomba into the British Victory Bombe (delivered March 1940, followed by the Agnus Dei Bombe in August 1940 with Gordon Welchman's diagonal board), built by Harold 'Doc' Keen at the British Tabulating Machine Company in Letchworth to test 17,576 three-rotor orientations against closed crib loops.
  • 7. Banburismus and the Weight of Evidence
    Punched Paper Sheets, Decibans, and Sequential Bayesian Inference Against Naval Enigma
    Turing and statistical assistant I. J. (Jack) Good in Hut 8 during 1941–1942 inventing Banburismus—punching intercepted German Kriegsmarine M3 Enigma ciphertexts onto long Banbury-printed paper sheets to score letter coincidences—and defining the logarithmic 'ban' and 'deciban' units of sequential Bayesian odds to identify shared Naval Enigma rotor wheels before running the Bombes.
  • 8. Delilah and the Bell Labs Tea-Time Encounter
    Portable Speech Scrambling at Hanslope Park and Meeting Claude Shannon in Manhattan
    Turing sailing to New York aboard the troopship RMS Queen Elizabeth in November 1942, inspecting the 55-ton SIGSALY (Green Hornet) voice scrambler and meeting Claude Shannon daily at Bell Telephone Laboratories in early 1943, and returning to Britain to build the portable thirty-valve Delilah speech scrambler with Donald Bayley at Hanslope Park in 1943–1945.
  • 9. The Automatic Computing Engine Blueprint
    Mercury Delay Lines, Micro-Coded Logic Tables, and the First Subroutine Stack
    Turing joining the Mathematics Division of the National Physical Laboratory in Teddington under John Womersley in October 1945 and submitting his December 1945 report 'Proposed Electronic Calculator' for the Automatic Computing Engine (ACE), specifying 1 MHz clock timing, five-foot mercury acoustic delay lines holding 1,024 bits, optimum coding, and hardware BURY/UNBURY subroutine stack operations.
  • 10. The Imitation Game and Lady Lovelace's Objection
    Why Playing Chess, Learning From Mistakes, and Conversing Replaced Definitions of Thought
    Turing's October 1950 paper 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence' in the philosophical journal Mind, replacing the question 'Can machines think?' with a three-participant teleprinter imitation game, systematically refuting nine philosophical and mathematical objections including Ada Lovelace's 1843 note on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, and proposing the training of a child machine.
  • 11. The Matrix Condition Number and LU Decomposition
    Inventing Modern Numerical Linear Algebra to Measure Round-Off Error
    Turing's 1948 paper 'Rounding-Off Errors in Matrix Processes' in the Quarterly Journal of Mechanics and Applied Mathematics, formalizing the PA = LU lower-upper triangular matrix factorization with partial row pivoting, defining the matrix condition number M(A) and N(A) (now written kappa(A)), and overturning pessimistic round-off error fears about Gaussian elimination.
  • 12. The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis
    How Two Diffusing Molecules Carve Leopard Spots, Zebra Stripes, and Fibonacci Spirals
    Turing's August 1952 paper 'The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis' in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London (Series B), deriving coupled reaction-diffusion partial differential equations for morphogens X and Y with unequal diffusion coefficients, computing spotted patterns on the Ferranti Mark 1 at the University of Manchester, and studying phyllotaxis Fibonacci spirals in Wilmslow between 1952 and June 1954.
