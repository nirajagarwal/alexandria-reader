The Ocean · Chapter 3

The Sunlit Epipelagic Zone

Prochlorococcus Cyanobacteria, Photic Attenuation, and the Invisible Forest

Pure water is intrinsically blue rather than colorless. Vibrational overtones of the oxygen-hydrogen covalent bond absorb red and orange wavelengths within the top 15 meters of the sea. Only narrow-band 470-nanometer blue light penetrates clear subtropical water down to 200 meters, turning the lower sunlit zone into a monochromatic blue world.

· · ·

Until 1988, oceanographers missed the most abundant photosynthetic organism on Earth because it passed right through plankton nets. Aiming a shipboard laser flow cytometer at seawater, Sallie Chisholm and Robert Olson discovered Prochlorococcus—a 0.6-micrometer cyanobacterium. Numbering three octillion ($3 \times 10^{27}$) cells globally, this tiny microbe generates up to 20 percent of biospheric oxygen.

· · ·

All marine phytoplankton combined hold 1 gigaton of carbon—just 0.2 percent of the 450 gigatons locked in land plants. Yet this microscopic forest fixes 50 gigatons of carbon every year, matching all terrestrial vegetation. Marine algae achieve that output because the entire standing crop divides and gets eaten every two to six days.

· · ·

Single-celled diatoms generate one-fifth of Earth's oxygen while encased in two-piece pillboxes called frustules made of hydrated silica glass ($\text{SiO}_2 \cdot n\text{H}_2\text{O}$). Although opal glass is denser than water, diatoms honeycomb their shells with nanometer pore lattices. These pores cut sinking weight, focus blue-green light onto chloroplasts, and resist copepod jaws.

· · ·

G. Evelyn Hutchinson posed the Paradox of the Plankton in 1961. Competitive exclusion theory states that when dozens of algal species compete for the same four dissolved nutrients in well-mixed water, one top competitor should drive all others extinct. Yet hundreds of species coexist in one liter because turbulence and viruses constantly reset the race.

· · ·

In 1934, Alfred Redfield noticed a striking chemical rule across the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian oceans. Deep seawater everywhere holds dissolved nitrate and phosphate in a fixed 16-to-1 atomic ratio—matching the exact 106 carbon to 16 nitrogen to 1 phosphorus recipe inside living phytoplankton. Marine microbes actively buffer the chemistry of the sea.

· · ·

Across 20 percent of the ocean, surface waters stay rich in nitrate and phosphate all year yet remain biological deserts. John Martin proved in 1988 that these High-Nutrient, Low-Chlorophyll zones suffer from iron deficiency. Because ferric iron is insoluble in seawater, adding one gram of dissolved iron triggers blooms fixing up to 100,000 grams of carbon.

· · ·

The most abundant heterotrophic bacterium in the sea, Pelagibacter ubique (clade SAR11, one-third of all surface cells), dominates nutrient-poor gyres through genetic austerity. Its streamlined genome holds just 1,354 genes and zero non-coding junk DNA. To save scarce phosphorus in barren water, it replaces membrane phospholipids with sulfur lipids.

· · ·

A single milliliter of sunlit seawater holds one million bacteria and ten million bacteriophage viruses. Every day, marine phages infect and burst 20 to 40 percent of all ocean bacteria. This viral shunt spills 3 gigatons of dissolved carbon annually back into surface microbes rather than letting it pass up the food chain.

· · ·

For a one-millimeter copepod, slow feeding paddles through water that feels as viscous as honey ($Re \sim 1$). When a fish approaches and bends the copepod's antennal hairs by 10 nanometers, the crustacean snaps four pairs of legs in sequence. Vaulting at 500 body lengths per second, it punches into inertial flow ($Re \sim 1,000$).

· · ·

The Portuguese man o' war (Physalia physalis) is a colonial siphonophore that cannot swim and drifts by its gas-filled bladder sail. If every colony sailed at the same angle, a steady wind would beach the whole population on one shore. Instead, colonies grow dimorphic left- or right-handed sails that tack 45 degrees apart.

· · ·

Most fish are cold-blooded because venous blood passing through their gills dumps 100 percent of metabolic muscle heat into the water. Atlantic bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus) beat this loss by routing blood through a countercurrent web of arteries and veins (rete mirabile). Trapping 95 percent of heat, a bluefin keeps its muscles at 28 degrees Celsius in 7-degree water.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding

Empirical Payoff: Trapping 95 percent of heat, a bluefin keeps its muscles at 28 degrees Celsius in 7-degree water.
