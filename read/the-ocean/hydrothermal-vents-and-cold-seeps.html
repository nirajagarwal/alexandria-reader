The Ocean · Chapter 11

Hydrothermal Vents and Cold Seeps

Black Smoker Sulfide Chimneys, Riftia Symbionts, and Lost City Alkaline Towers

Searching for missing plate-tectonic heat along the Galápagos Rift in February 1977 at 2,500 meters depth, geophysicists John Corliss and Robert Ballard dove aboard DSV Alvin into a sunless basalt valley. Their headlights revealed lush gardens of two-meter red-plumed tubeworms and giant white clams. It was the first ecosystem proven to run on geothermal chemistry rather than sunlight.

· · ·

Discovered along the East Pacific Rise in 1979 by Fred Spiess and Ballard, black smoker chimneys blast jet-black fluid out at 350 to 407 degrees Celsius—hot enough to melt lead and Alvin's plastic thermometer. The water never boils into steam. Overlying pressure of 250 to 300 atmospheres holds seawater near or above its critical point ($407\,^\circ\text{C}$ at $298\text{ bar}$).

· · ·

Cold seawater percolating toward a magma chamber loses its magnesium and sulfate while its pH drops to 2.8, leaching iron, copper, zinc, and sulfide from hot basalt. When this 380-degree acidic brine hits 2-degree seawater, calcium sulfate (anhydrite) precipitates first because anhydrite becomes insoluble above 150 degrees. That reaction builds chimney walls 30 centimeters taller per day.

· · ·

Growing two meters tall without a mouth, gut, or anus, the giant vent tubeworm Riftia pachyptila baffled zoologists until graduate student Colleen Cavanaugh proved in 1981 that its internal trophosome organ houses billions of sulfur-oxidizing bacteria per gram. In pitch darkness, these symbionts oxidize hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) to drive the Calvin-Benson cycle and fix carbon into sugar.

· · ·

Hydrogen sulfide poisons animal mitochondria like cyanide by blocking cytochrome $c$ oxidase, yet Riftia must deliver both oxygen and sulfide to its bacterial organ without poisoning itself. Alissa Arp and George Somero showed in 1983 that Riftia's red blood contains giant hexagonal hemoglobins with separate binding sites. Iron hemes bind $\text{O}_2$ while cysteine thiols bind $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ reversibly.

· · ·

Burrowing into active black smoker chimney walls, the ten-centimeter Pompeii polychaete worm (Alvinella pompejana) endures the steepest thermal gradient of any animal. Thermocouple probes placed inside its tube by Craig Cary in 1998 recorded 80-degree water around the worm's tail while its gills poked into 22-degree water centimeters away. A dorsal fleece of symbiotic bacteria insulates the worm.

· · ·

Swarming across Mid-Atlantic Ridge chimneys at 3,500 meters, the vent shrimp Rimicaris exoculata lacks external stalked eyes. Yet Cindy Van Dover and Steven Chamberlain discovered in 1989 that a white rhodopsin patch sits beneath the shell on its neck. That organ detects the faint blackbody thermal glow of 350-degree chimneys so the shrimp can graze without cooking.

· · ·

Discovered at Indian Ocean vents in 2001, the scaly-foot snail (Chrysomallon squamiferum) is the only living animal that builds iron sulfide minerals into its skeletal armor. Christine Ortiz proved in 2010 that its shell is a three-layer composite. An outer coat of ferromagnetic greigite ($\text{Fe}_3\text{S}_4$) nanoparticles sits over spongy protein and inner aragonite to arrest cracks.

· · ·

Along Gulf of Mexico margins at 1,000 to 3,000 meters, tectonic compression squeezes cold methane, sulfide, and hypersaline brine upward from salt domes to form underwater lakes four times saltier than seawater. Cold seeps leak steady fuel for millennia. Consequently, Charles Fisher showed in 2000 that the seep tubeworm Lamellibrachia luymesi lives for 250 to 300 years.

· · ·

Below 500 meters depth at 2 degrees Celsius, upward-seeping methane gas locks water molecules into solid crystalline cages of white methane clathrate ice that fizz and ignite if brought to the surface. Colonizing these Gulf of Mexico gas hydrate mounds are pink polychaete ice worms (Hesiocauda methanicola, discovered in 1997). They graze methanotrophic bacteria off the ice.

· · ·

In December 2000, Deborah Kelley discovered the Lost City field 15 kilometers off the Mid-Atlantic Ridge: 60-meter-tall white carbonate towers driven not by magma, but by exothermic serpentinization of mantle olivine rock with seawater. The reaction vents warm (40 to 90 degrees Celsius), strongly alkaline (pH 9 to 11) fluid rich in hydrogen gas and abiotic methane.

· · ·

Biochemists Michael Russell, William Martin, and Nick Lane point to Lost City–style alkaline vents as the cradle of life four billion years ago. When pH-10, hydrogen-rich vent fluid bubbles through iron-nickel-sulfur mineral pores into an ancient pH-6 carbon-dioxide ocean, a natural 200-millivolt proton gradient sits across the pore wall. That gradient matches the ATP synthase proton pump of all life.
