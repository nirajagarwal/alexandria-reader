Radical Subtraction · Chapter 2

The Anchorhold of Norwich

Julian of Norwich and the Confinement of the Hazelnuts

Attached to the south stone wall of St. Julian's Church in Norwich stood a sealed chamber measuring roughly twelve feet square. Its exterior timber door had been mortared shut with flint and lime. The room possessed only three small apertures. An angled squint cut through the thick interior masonry allowed the occupant to view the parish altar. A shuttered hatch opened into an attendant's anteroom for passing bread and removing waste. A small barred window hung with a black wool curtain faced the public street. Inside lived a woman whose birth name vanished from the municipal records, known solely as Julian after the church to whose wall she was bricked.

In May 1373, at the age of thirty, she had lain on her bed dying of an acute febrile illness while her curate held a brass crucifix before her failing eyes. Over forty-eight hours of fever and peripheral hypoxia, her visual and conceptual fields locked onto sixteen vivid perceptual sequences before the fever broke on May 13. Rather than returning to ordinary household life in a trading city of ten thousand inhabitants, she petitioned the Bishop of Norwich for permanent enclosure as a solitary anchoress. During the induction rite, the bishop chanted the Office of the Dead over her prostrate body, scattered grave dust across her veil, stepped out of the cell, and ordered masons to seal the doorway. Under medieval canon law, she was legally dead to the world. She remained inside that twelve-foot cube for more than forty years until her death around 1416.

This architectural confinement functioned as a four-decade perceptual reduction experiment. In sensory neuroscience, the primate visual cortex divides processing between two pathways. The dorsal stream computes rapid spatial navigation, motor reach, and optic flow across changing environments. The ventral stream integrates form, semantic meaning, and long-term episodic memory. When an observer's physical surroundings are held invariant inside a twelve-by-twelve stone room across decades, dorsal-stream spatial updating drops to a minimal baseline. Metabolic resources reallocate toward deep conceptual synthesis. While recurring waves of pneumonic and bubonic plague killed nearly half the population of Norwich outside her wall in 1375, 1383, and 1390, Julian spent twenty years dissecting the neurological and philosophical structure of her sixteen visions. She drafted two successive analytical versions—the Short Text and the Long Text of Revelations of Divine Love—producing the earliest surviving books in the English language written by a woman.

Her sharpest perceptual reduction examined the contingency of physical scale. In the first vision, she perceived a small spherical object resting in the palm of her hand, no larger than a common hazelnut. When she inquired internally what this minute sphere represented, she understood that it was the entirety of created existence. Every star, ocean, kingdom, and century was compressed into a single round mass. She noted that the sphere looked fragile enough to collapse into non-existence at any instant because of its sheer smallness, yet it endured continuously because the underlying ground of reality sustained it.

In Julian's spatial compression, macroscopic size confers zero ontological privilege. By shrinking the visible universe to the angular diameter of a nut on human skin, she stripped away the intimidation of historical catastrophe. When plague survivors and the mystic Margery Kempe traveled to Norwich in 1413 to speak with Julian through the black curtain of her street window, the anchoress offered no apocalyptic warnings. Having watched forty years of epidemic mortality from a room she could never leave, she concluded her manuscript with the quiet observation that every contingent fracture in time would resolve into order.
