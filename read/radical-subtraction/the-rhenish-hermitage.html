The Rhenish Hermitage

Hildegard of Bingen and the Scivias Telemetry at Disibodenberg

The thirty-five illuminated plates of the Rupertsberg manuscript of Scivias, painted under the direct supervision of Hildegard of Bingen between 1141 and 1151, depict concentric rings of blazing gold fire, jagged crenelated battlements, and showers of brilliant sparks fading into black char. In 1970, when clinical neurologist Oliver Sacks compared those twelfth-century parchment illuminations against hospital sketches drawn by twentieth-century neurology patients, he identified the unmistakable geometric signature of classical migraine aura: fortification spectra (teichopsia) and scintillating scotomas.

In the autumn of 1141, Hildegard sat inside the stone anchorhold attached to the Benedictine abbey of Disibodenberg above the Nahe River in the German Rhineland. Placed into the monastic enclosure at the age of eight as a tithe by her noble parents and bricked in beside the older recluse Jutta of Sponheim, she had lived behind those stone walls for thirty-four years. Since her third year of life, she had experienced recurring episodes of blinding geometric luminance—which she termed umbra viventis lucis, the reflection of the living light. These visual displays were accompanied by temporary blindness, paresthesia, and weeks of severe physical immobilization. Crucially, unlike the dissociative trances reported by later medieval ecstatics, Hildegard never lost waking consciousness during an episode. She observed the luminous geometric arrays superimposed directly across her open-eyed visual field while dictating and conversing in her cell.

In modern neurophysiology, visual migraine aura is generated by cortical spreading depression (CSD), first measured by Brazilian neurophysiologist Aristides Leão in 1944. CSD is a self-propagating wave of intense neuronal and glial depolarization followed by transient electrical silence. It crawls across the grey matter of the primary visual cortex (area V1) in the occipital lobe at a constant velocity:

$$v_{\text{CSD}} \approx 2 \text{ to } 5 \text{ mm/min}$$

Because the retinotopic map of the human visual field is laid out across the calcarine sulcus of V1 in orientation-selective hypercolumns, a three-millimeter-per-minute wave of extracellular potassium ($K^+$) and glutamate efflux fires those columns sequentially. That advancing wave creates an expanding crescent of flickering, angled zig-zag lines. Those shimmering geometric walls closely resemble the fortified bastions of a medieval citadel. Immediately behind the depolarizing wavefront trails a temporary scotoma (blind zone) that clears after twenty to sixty minutes as ATP-dependent sodium-potassium pumps restore transmembrane ion gradients.

For thirty-four years inside the Disibodenberg anchorhold, fearing her visual anomalies would be diagnosed as demonic delusion, Hildegard kept the phenomena secret. In 1141, following a prolonged debilitating illness after she was elected magistra of the nuns, she began recording her visual observations onto wax tablets with the grammatical assistance of the monk Volmar. When Pope Eugenius III examined partial drafts of Scivias at the Synod of Trier in November 1147 and read passages aloud to the assembled cardinals, he granted formal papal authorization for her to write.

Authorized by Rome, Hildegard converted the raw electrophysiological telemetry of her occipital cortex into unprecedented institutional autonomy. Over the next three decades, she completed three volumes of visionary cosmology alongside two encyclopedic treatises on empirical medicine and natural history (Physica and Causae et Curae). Those medical volumes cataloged more than two hundred Rhenish medicinal plants, minerals, and river fish using vernacular German terms. They also prescribed boiled water for washing wounds and hops as an antimicrobial preservative. She composed seventy-seven liturgical chants spanning two-and-a-half octaves that shattered the narrow modal conventions of Gregorian plainchant, and she constructed a twenty-three-letter cipher alphabet (Lingua Ignota). In 1150, she defied the abbot of Disibodenberg and moved her nuns into an independent monastery at Rupertsberg engineered with gravity-fed indoor running water.
