Radical Subtraction · Chapter 4

The Four-Cornered Negation

Nagarjuna and the Dismantling of Independent Essence at Nalanda

During the five centuries following the death of Siddhartha Gautama, early Buddhist scholastic schools across India—most notably the Sarvastivadins and Sautrantikas—attempted to explain experience by breaking reality down into an exhaustive periodic table of fundamental building blocks called dharmas. While these scholastics agreed that macroscopic objects like chariots, forests, and human personalities were temporary assemblies with no permanent soul, they insisted that the microscopic constituent elements—fleeting instants of color, tactile pressure, and mental sensation—possessed their own irreducible, self-existing intrinsic nature (svabhava). Around 150 CE in South India, a monk and logician named Nagarjuna demolished this entire atomic edifice in a four-hundred-and-forty-eight-verse Sanskrit treatise titled the Mulamadhyamakakarika (Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way).

Nagarjuna identified a fatal logical contradiction at the heart of intrinsic essence. By definition, any entity possessing svabhava must exist independently by its own power, unconditioned by anything outside itself and immune to change. Yet everything observed in the empirical world arises in dependence on prior conditions, interacts with neighboring phenomena, and eventually ceases. If a seed possessed an immutable intrinsic essence of seed-ness, it could never transform into a sprout. Conversely, if the sprout already existed inside the seed, no moisture, soil, or sunlight would be needed to produce it.

To strip away the illusion of independent essence across every category of thought, Nagarjuna deployed an ancient Indian dialectical razor known as the catuskoti, or four-cornered negation (tetralemma). For any proposed metaphysical claim $X$—whether concerning causation, motion, time, or the self—Nagarjuna systematically refuted all four exhaustive logical possibilities:

  • First Corner — Affirmation ($X$): Things do not arise out of themselves (svatah*), because if an effect already exists inside its own cause, further production is redundant and causal change would never stop.
  • Second Corner — Negation ($\neg X$): Things do not arise from something completely other (paratah*), because if cause and effect share no intrinsic connection, anything could produce anything—darkness could generate light and a stone could sprout wheat.
  • * Third Corner — Conjunction ($X \land \neg X$): Things do not arise from both self and other simultaneously, because conjoining two incoherent models simply inherits the fatal contradictions of both halves.
  • Fourth Corner — Disjunction ($\neg(X \lor \neg X)$): Things do not arise from neither—that is, without any cause at all (ahetutah*)—because uncaused randomness would sever all empirical regularity between effort and outcome.

Walking this four-cornered blade through twenty-seven chapters—examining visual perception, physical motion, temporal past-present-future divisions, and the historical Buddha himself—Nagarjuna left not a single self-existing atom standing. His analysis of motion in Chapter 2 illustrates the precision of the cut. The path already traversed is not currently being traversed, and the path not yet reached is not being traversed either. Apart from the already-traversed and the not-yet-traversed, no third static point of present motion can be isolated without freezing movement into rest.

Crucially, Nagarjuna warned that his subtraction of essence (sunyata, or emptiness) must never be mistaken for nihilism or converted into a new metaphysical substance. Emptiness is not a vacuum hiding behind phenomena; it is the exact logical equivalent of dependent co-arising (pratityasamutpada). Because nothing possesses frozen, independent self-nature, everything is free to arise, interact, and transform through relational networks. To cling to emptiness as an affirmative theory, Nagarjuna observed in Chapter 13, is like seizing a venomous water snake by the wrong end—an incurable error that turns a purgative medicine into poison. In the twenty-fourth chapter of his treatise, which became the foundational curriculum at the great monastic university of Nalanda for seven hundred years, Nagarjuna demonstrated that if things possessed immutable intrinsic essence, no action could alter the world and no suffering could ever be ended, whereas only emptiness allows causes and conditions to function.
