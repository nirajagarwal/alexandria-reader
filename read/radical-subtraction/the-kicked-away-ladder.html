Radical Subtraction · Chapter 12

The Kicked-Away Ladder

Ludwig Wittgenstein and Where Logical Language Draws Its Own Border

While serving as an artillery spotter and searchlight operator in the Austro-Hungarian army on the Eastern and Italian fronts between 1914 and 1918, Ludwig Wittgenstein carried small lined notebooks inside his military rucksack. Under artillery bombardment along the Vistula River and in the mountain trenches of Asiago, the former Cambridge engineering student distilled a seventy-five-page manuscript organized into seven decimal-numbered propositions. Completed when he was taken prisoner by Italian troops near Trento in November 1918 and published in 1921 as the Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung (Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus), it remains the sharpest linguistic subtraction in modern philosophy.

Wittgenstein's breakthrough began in the autumn of 1914 when he read a magazine report about a Paris traffic court trial. To reconstruct a collision between an automobile and a pedestrian, lawyers had arranged miniature wooden carts, toy figures, and scale houses on a table in front of the magistrate. Wittgenstein realized that the courtroom scale model could represent the street accident only because the spatial arrangement of the miniature objects shared the exact same logical multiplicity—the same structural degrees of freedom—as the real vehicles on the Paris street.

From that courtroom table, he derived the picture theory of language. Every meaningful proposition is a logical picture (Bild) of a possible state of affairs in the empirical world. Words name simple objects, and the syntactic configuration of those words mirrors how objects stand in relation to one another. Consequently, the total set of meaningful human propositions is strictly isomorphic to the total set of contingent empirical facts—the domain of the natural sciences.

This structural isomorphism imposes an absolute mathematical boundary. A picture can depict any spatial or logical arrangement of objects inside the world, but it cannot step outside itself to depict the pictorial form that makes representation possible. The human eye sees everything inside the visual field, yet nothing inside the visual field proves that it is seen by an eye. Whenever philosophers or theologians attempt to construct propositions about the meaning of life, ethical value, aesthetic beauty, or the existence of the world as a whole, they violate the syntax of representation. Their sentences are not deep truths; they are structural nonsense (Unsinn) produced by pushing language past its operating boundary.

When Wittgenstein mailed the finished typescript from his prisoner-of-war camp at Cassino to Bertrand Russell and later to the Vienna publisher Ludwig von Ficker in October 1919, he explained in a letter to Ficker that the book's true center was its unwritten second half. While traditional moralists tried to protect ethics and value by babbling about them in pseudo-scientific prose, Wittgenstein drew the boundary of factual language from the inside. He established what cannot be said by exhaustively mapping everything that can be said.

In Proposition 6.54, immediately before the final sentence of the book, Wittgenstein turned this subtractive blade against his own treatise. He stated that anyone who genuinely understood him would eventually recognize that the book's own numbered propositions were nonsensical scaffolding—steps on a ladder that the reader must climb up and then throw away once on top. True to that instruction, after the Tractatus was published, Wittgenstein gave away his entire inherited fortune in Vienna. He walked away from academic philosophy for a decade to work as an elementary school teacher in the mountain villages of Lower Austria and as a monastery gardener at Hütteldorf. Having used formal symbolic logic to clear away the entire history of metaphysical chatter, he closed the book with Proposition 7, instructing that where one cannot speak, one must pass over in silence.
