Radical Subtraction · Chapter 5

The Apophatic Cloud

The Anonymous English Contemplative and the Erasure of Memory

Somewhere in the East Midlands of England around 1375, an anonymous country priest drafted a seventy-five-chapter manual in vernacular Middle English for a young Carthusian student. He opened the manuscript with a strict operational warning: the book must never be read by clever talkers, curious academics, or anyone unwilling to sit quietly in mental darkness. The author left no personal name, monastery, or Latin title on the vellum, calling the work simply The Cloud of Unknowing.

For the preceding two centuries, European university theology had been dominated by the kataphatic—or affirmative—architecture of high scholasticism. Following Peter Lombard and Thomas Aquinas, masters at the universities of Paris and Oxford constructed monumental logical edifices, the Summae, by stacking positive propositions. They defined ultimate reality through chains of Aristotelian causes, syllogisms, moral attributes, and scriptural analogies.

The anonymous Midland author rejected the entire affirmative apparatus as a category error. Human language and conceptual imagination, he argued, evolved to manipulate finite physical objects in three-dimensional space—tools, food, territorial boundaries, and social hierarchies. Whenever the human intellect attempts to capture unconditioned reality using mental pictures or verbal definitions, it fabricates a cognitive idol. Accumulating subtler theological arguments does not bring the observer closer to reality. It merely adds higher-frequency static to the channel.

To clear the mental workspace, the manual prescribes a two-layer attentional filter:

  • * The Cloud of Forgetting: The practitioner must press every autobiographical memory, every sensory image, every philosophical distinction, and even pious visualizations of biblical scenes down beneath a dense lower barrier of forgetting. Whenever a clever analysis or past grievance surfaces, the student must step on it immediately before it links into an associative narrative chain.
  • The Cloud of Unknowing: Above the practitioner hangs a featureless dark boundary where analytical categorization cannot function. Rather than attempting to penetrate this darkness with discursive thought, the practitioner directs bare, non-verbal focal attention against it. To prevent the verbal mind from restarting, the student anchors attention onto a single monosyllabic word—such as God or Love*—used not as a concept to analyze, but as a rhythmic phonetic shield.

In modern cognitive neuroscience, this dual-cloud protocol directly targets the recurrent loop between the hippocampus and the left inferior frontal gyrus. The hippocampus continuously retrieves episodic memories from storage, while Broca's area in the frontal cortex serializes those retrievals into inner speech. In predictive processing models of the brain, the Default Mode Network relies on continuous token-to-token linguistic transitions to maintain its internal simulation of the ego. When a practitioner suppresses multi-word syntax and restricts the phonological loop to a single unelaborated syllable, the predictive cascade starves for transitions and falls silent.

The Midland author was fiercely skeptical of visionary trances and somatic heat. In Chapters 45 through 57, he observed how undisciplined novices twisted their necks, stared with bulging eyes as if peering after sheep, or strained their abdominal muscles to manufacture artificial interior sensations. He warned his student never to interpret the darkness or the cloud literally as atmospheric vapor or the absence of candle light. Students who strained their ocular muscles to stare into an imagined interior blackness merely inflamed their own nervous systems and generated neurotic delusions. When the manuscript circulated among English Carthusian Charterhouses such as Mount Grace Priory in the fifteenth century, solitary monks copied the text without attribution, preserving a stripped-down mechanical manual for shutting off the brain's narrative generator.
