Self-Transcendence · Chapter 7

The Evolutionary Puzzle of Self-Sacrifice

Darwin's Tribe Dilemma, the Price Equation, and Why Nature Built Creatures That Die for Strangers

In the fifth chapter of The Descent of Man, published in February 1871, Charles Darwin confronted a biological anomaly that threatened his theory of natural selection. Natural selection is an unforgiving filter of differential survival and reproduction. Any heritable trait that helps an individual survive and leave more offspring spreads through the population, while any trait that lowers individual survival is weeded out. Why, Darwin asked, do human beings possess moral instincts that drive a soldier to throw himself onto a spear for his comrades or a stranger to leap into a river to rescue a drowning child?

Inside any single tribe, Darwin noted with candor, the brave, sympathetic, and self-sacrificing person is far more likely to perish young on the battlefield or in a crisis than the cautious, calculating egoist who hangs back in the rear. Left to individual competition within a group, noble self-transcendence should breed itself out of existence within a few generations. The earth would be populated exclusively by cowards and cheats.

Yet Darwin immediately saw the second half of the ledger. While a selfish individual defeats an altruist inside the same village, a tribe composed of courageous, loyal, self-sacrificing members ready to warn one another of danger and defend the common good will defeat and supplant a rival tribe of squabbling egoists. Human evolution, Darwin proposed, had been shaped on two levels at once. For a century after 1871, however, biologists lacked the mathematical tools to calculate when between-group cooperation could overcome within-group selfishness. By the 1960s, led by George C. Williams and William D. Hamilton's gene-centric models of kin selection, orthodox biology declared group selection dead, insisting that apparent selflessness was disguised genetic nepotism.

The man who solved Darwin's two-level puzzle was not a biologist, but an American physical chemist and former Manhattan Project analyst named George Robert Price. Moving to London in November 1967 after thyroid cancer surgery left his shoulder partially paralyzed, the militant atheist and former IBM consultant read Hamilton's papers on kin selection in Bloomsbury libraries. Perturbed by the implication that genuine self-transcendence could not exist in nature, Price sat down in his rented flat near Oxford Street and derived a fundamental covariance theorem from scratch. When he walked unannounced into the office of statistical geneticist Cedric Smith at University College London's Galton Laboratory in 1968 and laid the two-page derivation on the desk, Smith saw within an hour that the unknown American had discovered the universal algebra of evolutionary change.

Published in Nature in 1970 and extended in 1972, the Price Equation partitions evolutionary change in the average value of any trait $\Delta \bar{z}$ across a generation into two additive terms—selection between groups of fitness $w_g$ and trait mean $z_g$, plus expected selection within each group $k$:

$$\Delta \bar{z} = \operatorname{Cov}(w_g, z_g) + E\left[\operatorname{Cov}_k(w_{ik}, z_{ik})\right]$$

For an altruistic, self-transcending trait $z$, the second term on the right—within-group covariance—is always negative, because inside any group $k$, self-sacrificing individuals suffer lower personal fitness $w_{ik}$ than free-riders. That is the cynical half of nature. Yet the first term—the covariance between a group's collective survival $w_g$ and its fraction of altruists $z_g$—is strongly positive whenever cohesive groups out-survive selfish groups. Price's equation proved that self-transcendence evolves whenever between-group variance in cooperation is large enough to outweigh within-group exploitation.

During the Pleistocene epoch, human bands acquired cultural norms, shared rituals, and collective punishment of bullies that suppressed within-group free-riding while amplifying differences between rival groups. As evolutionary biologist David Sloan Wilson and psychologist Jonathan Haidt synthesized, multilevel selection wired the human brain as a hybrid creature. Humans act like self-serving primates when competing for status inside a hierarchy, yet carry an ancient neurobiological switch that temporarily shuts down egoic calculation to merge the individual into a cohesive superorganism during moments of shared awe, rhythm, or collective survival.

For George Price, the discovery that altruism could exist only through a fragile balance against selfishness triggered a personal reckoning. Converting from militant atheism to a literal reading of the New Testament in 1970, Price resolved to test whether a human could defy the second term of his own equation through unconditional generosity. Shortly after deriving the exact mathematical equation showing how selfless sacrifice evolves when between-group variance outweighs within-group selfishness, George Price gave away all his money, furniture, and winter coat to homeless strangers in central London until he was sleeping on the floor of a squatter's room near Euston Station.
