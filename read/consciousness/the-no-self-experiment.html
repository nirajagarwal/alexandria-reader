Consciousness · Chapter 7

The No-Self Experiment

King Milinda's Chariot, the Upanishadic Witness, and Ten Thousand Hours on a Meditation Cushion

For the past three chapters, we have looked at consciousness from the outside—through scalp sensors, magnetic coils, and hospital scanners. Yet long before anyone built an MRI machine, contemplative traditions in India, Tibet, China, and Japan spent more than two thousand years investigating consciousness from the inside through disciplined first-person observation.

When Western philosophers like René Descartes looked inward in the seventeenth century, they concluded that the single most certain thing in the universe is the solid, permanent ego (I think, therefore I am). When early Buddhist and Hindu contemplatives sat quietly and watched their own minds breath by breath, they reported something radically different.

In a famous first-century BCE dialogue called the Milinda Panha (Questions of King Milinda), the Greek-Bactrian king Menander asked the Buddhist sage Nagasena who he was. Nagasena asked the king how he had arrived at the monastery—on foot or in a chariot? When the king pointed to his royal chariot, Nagasena asked him to point to the chariot itself. Is the chariot the wooden wheels? The axle? The reins? The yoke? Or is "chariot" just a convenient everyday label we give to a temporary assembly of parts? In the exact same way, Buddhist psychology argued under the principle of anatta (no-self) that when you pay close attention to your own mind, you never find a permanent little CEO sitting behind your eyes—only a fast-moving river of sensations, thoughts, feelings, and habits arising and passing away:

  • The Chariot and the River (Anatta)*: Just as a movie projector flashes twenty-four still frames per second so quickly that they look like a solid moving object, ordinary untrained attention blurs rapid bursts of sensation and inner chatter into the illusion of a permanent ego.
  • The Four States and the Pure Witness (Turiya): Around the same era, the Mandukya Upanishad in the Advaita Vedanta tradition mapped human consciousness across four states: waking life (Jagrat), dreaming sleep (Svapna), deep dreamless sleep (Sushupti), and a fourth underlying canvas called Turiya. In this view, Turiya* is pure, quiet witness awareness itself, which remains present even when all thoughts and ego-stories fall silent.
  • * Ten thousand hours in the scanner (2004–2011): What happens inside the physical brain when a human being practices that inward attention for decades? Neuroscientists Richard Davidson and Antoine Lutz at the University of Wisconsin wired Tibetan Buddhist monks—such as molecular biologist turned monk Matthieu Ricard, each with 10,000 to 50,000 hours of meditation—to 256 scalp sensors, while Judson Brewer at Yale scanned experienced meditators inside fMRI tubes.

Two dramatic brain shifts appeared in the data. First, even while resting between meditation sessions, the monks' brains generated massive, high-amplitude 40-hertz gamma waves synchronized across distant brain regions—the strongest continuous neural synchrony ever recorded in healthy non-epileptic brains. When a startling loud gunshot sound was played through headphones, the monks showed almost no involuntary startle jump. Second, inside the fMRI scanner, experienced meditators showed a sharp drop in activity inside the Default Mode Network. That midline brain hub normally chatters away whenever we daydream, worry about our reputation, or replay stories about me, myself, and I. What ancient contemplatives described as quieting the ego turned out to have a measurable signature in the living brain.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In the first-century BCE Buddhist dialogue Milinda Panha, the sage Nagasena asked King Milinda whether a royal chariot is the wheels, the axle, the yoke, or the reins—demonstrating two millennia before modern neuroscience that what we call the 'self' is a shifting bundle of parts with no tiny driver sitting inside.
Empirical Payoff: When neuroscientist Richard Davidson and Antoine Lutz wired Tibetan Buddhist monks with more than 10,000 to 50,000 hours of meditation practice to 256 scalp sensors at the University of Wisconsin in 2004, the monks generated the highest-amplitude continuous gamma-wave synchrony ever recorded in healthy human brains while quieting the brain's wandering ego network.
