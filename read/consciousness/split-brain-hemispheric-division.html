Consciousness · Chapter 4

Split-Brain Hemispheric Division

How Severing Two Hundred Million Axons Created Two Independent Streams of Awareness

Two hundred million myelinated nerve fibers form the corpus callosum, the thick white-matter bridge connecting the left and right cerebral hemispheres of the human brain. In 1962, Los Angeles neurosurgeons Philip Vogel and Joseph Bogen severed the entire corpus callosum and anterior commissure of a World War II paratrooper known by the initials W.J. The patient had suffered from intractable grand mal epileptic seizures for fifteen years after wartime head trauma. The operation halted his generalized convulsions. In casual conversation and standard bedside neurological exams, W.J. appeared completely unchanged in personality, humor, and intellect.

At the California Institute of Technology, however, neurobiologist Roger Sperry and his graduate student Michael Gazzaniga built a divided-field tachistoscope to test W.J.'s two hemispheres separately. Because nasal retinal fibers cross at the optic chiasm, every visual stimulus located to the left of a central fixation point projects exclusively to the right visual cortex. Every stimulus to the right of fixation projects exclusively to the left visual cortex. In a healthy person, the corpus callosum shares both halves of the visual scene across the midline in milliseconds. And if an image stays on a screen for more than two hundred milliseconds, a split-brain patient simply flicks their eyes in a rapid saccade so both hemispheres see it. Sperry and Gazzaniga defeated eye movements by flashing images for just one hundred milliseconds—faster than the oculomotor system can initiate a saccade.

When Gazzaniga flashed a photograph of a spoon to W.J.'s right visual field, the signal entered the left hemisphere. Because the left hemisphere houses Broca's and Wernicke's speech centers in over ninety-five percent of right-handed people, W.J. immediately said he saw a spoon. When a picture of a key was flashed to his left visual field, however, the signal entered only the disconnected right hemisphere. Asked what he had seen, W.J.'s speaking left hemisphere sincerely reported seeing nothing at all, or merely an empty white flash. Yet when Gazzaniga asked W.J. to reach beneath an opaque curtain with his left hand—controlled by the right hemisphere's motor cortex—and select the matching object by touch, his left hand bypassed the spoon and picked up the key every time. Asked what his left hand was holding behind the screen, W.J.'s speaking hemisphere had to guess at random.

Even more revealing were the simultaneous double-picture experiments conducted in the late 1970s by Gazzaniga and neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux on commissurotomy patient P.S. Unlike W.J., P.S. had a right hemisphere with enough receptive vocabulary to comprehend written nouns despite being unable to speak. On one trial, the researchers flashed a picture of a chicken claw to P.S.'s left hemisphere while simultaneously flashing a winter snow scene to his right hemisphere. Placed before an array of choice cards, P.S.'s right hand pointed to a chicken while his left hand pointed to a snow shovel.

Asked why his two hands had chosen two unrelated items, P.S. never admitted that he did not know why his left hand picked the shovel. Without hesitation, his verbal left hemisphere—which had seen only the chicken claw and watched the left hand touch the shovel—explained that the chicken claw went with the chicken, and a person needs a shovel to clean out the chicken shed. Gazzaniga named this left-hemisphere system the interpreter: a narrative module that continuously observes the body's actions and fabricates coherent post-hoc explanations for behaviors initiated outside its own conscious awareness. When Gazzaniga and LeDoux flashed the written command to walk exclusively to the left visual field of patient P.S. in 1977, his mute right hemisphere read the word and caused him to stand up and walk toward the testing-room door, whereupon his verbal left hemisphere—asked why he had risen—immediately claimed that he was going to get a cold drink.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: Roger Sperry, Michael Gazzaniga, Joseph Bogen, and Philip Vogel's 1962 Caltech commissurotomy studies on patient W.J., Gazzaniga and Joseph LeDoux's 1977–1978 studies of patient P.S., and Sperry's 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
Empirical Payoff: When Michael Gazzaniga and Joseph LeDoux flashed the command 'walk' exclusively to the left visual field of commissurotomy patient P.S. in 1977, his mute right hemisphere read the word and caused him to stand up and walk toward the testing-room door, whereupon his verbal left hemisphere—asked why he had risen—immediately claimed that he was going to get a cold drink.
