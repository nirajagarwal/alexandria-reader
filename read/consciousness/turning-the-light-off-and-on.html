Consciousness · Chapter 6

Turning the Light Off and On

Anesthesia, Locked-In Tennis Players, and the Secret Signal From Inside a Lucid Dream

Every day in hospital operating rooms around the world, anesthesiologists perform a routine miracle. They inject a milky liquid like propofol into a patient's vein, and within thirty seconds, the person's conscious awareness vanishes completely. The patient feels no scalpel, forms no memories, and experiences zero passage of time until the drug is turned off and they wake up asking when the surgery will start.

For over a century, doctors assumed general anesthetics worked by turning the brain's neurons off into silence. Then MIT neuroscientist Emery Brown recorded electrical waves inside patients' brains during anesthesia and found the opposite. Under propofol, neurons do not go silent. Instead, the thalamus and the frontal cortex lock into a loud, slow, rhythmic 10-hertz chant. Like a stadium crowd chanting a single word in unison, that synchronized drumbeat makes it impossible for different brain regions to send detailed messages back and forth, temporarily disconnecting the brain's telephone switchboard.

What happens when a brain injury leaves a patient lying in a hospital bed with eyes open, unable to speak or move a single finger? How can doctors tell whether the person inside that silent body is completely unconscious in a vegetative state, or fully awake and trapped inside a paralyzed body?

In 2006 at the University of Cambridge, neuroscientist Adrian Owen placed a young woman who had been diagnosed as vegetative for five months after a car crash inside an fMRI brain scanner. While she lay motionless in the tube, Owen spoke to her through headphones and gave her two mental tasks. First, he asked her to imagine standing on a tennis court swinging a racket back and forth at a ball. Instantly, the motor strip at the top of her brain lit up on the screen. Next, he asked her to imagine walking from room to room through her front door at home. Her brain's spatial navigation hub lit up in the exact same pattern as healthy volunteers. By using mental tennis for yes and walking through the house for no, Owen later enabled completely paralyzed patients to answer yes-or-no questions using only their thoughts.

To build a bedside Consciousness Meter that does not require a patient to understand spoken English, Italian neuroscientist Marcello Massimini invented the "zap-and-zip" test, or Perturbational Complexity Index ($\text{PCI}$). Doctors hold a magnetic coil over the skull to tap the cortex like a bell, record the electrical echo across the brain with an EEG cap, and measure how complex the echo is by dividing the compressed file size $C(L)$ by the raw signal entropy $H(L)$:

$$\text{PCI} = \frac{C(L)}{L \cdot H(L)}$$

In deep dreamless sleep or anesthesia, the magnetic tap produces a simple, local thud ($\text{PCI} < 0.31$). In an awake, dreaming, or locked-in conscious brain, the tap rings across the whole cortex in a complex, shifting wave ($\text{PCI} > 0.31$) with 100 percent accuracy.

Science has even opened a two-way radio line into dreams. During REM dreaming, your brainstem paralyzes all your body muscles so you do not act out your dreams—except the muscles that move your eyeballs. In 1978 at Stanford University, sleep researcher Stephen LaBerge agreed on a secret signal before going to sleep. The moment he realized he was dreaming inside a dream, he moved his eyes left-right-left-right four times in a row, sending the first live Morse code message from the dream world onto the laboratory chart recorder.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In 2006 at the University of Cambridge, neuroscientist Adrian Owen placed a young woman diagnosed as being in a vegetative state after a car crash inside an fMRI scanner and asked her to imagine playing tennis or walking through her house—and her brain lit up in the exact same pattern as healthy volunteers.
Empirical Payoff: In 1978 at Stanford University, sleep researcher Stephen LaBerge proved that lucid dreamers are truly conscious inside their dreams by agreeing on a secret eye-movement Morse code before bedtime—and then moving his eyes left-right-left-right on the lab recording chart while dreaming that he was flying.
