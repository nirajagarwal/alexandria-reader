Consciousness · Chapter 10

The Private Architecture of Thought

Modernist Streams, Congenital Aphantasia, and Julian Jaynes's Bicameral Mind

In 1880, the British polymath Francis Galton mailed a printed questionnaire to one hundred distinguished fellows of the Royal Society—mathematicians, physicists, and engineers—asking them to sit down, call to mind their own breakfast table from that morning, and rate the brightness, color, and sharpness of the mental picture before their mind's eye. Galton expected every human mind to work like a camera obscura. When the replies arrived, he was astonished. Many of Britain's foremost scientists protested that the very phrase mental image was a figure of speech: they knew perfectly well what they had eaten for breakfast, yet the inside of their head was completely dark, containing zero visual pictures at all.

Because subjective experience is locked inside the first person, human beings suffer from a lifelong projection bias: we assume that the private texture of our own thoughts is universal human nature. In Chapter IX of his 1890 Principles of Psychology, William James rejected the older British empiricist view that consciousness is built like a train of discrete mental bricks, coining the phrase the stream of thought (or stream of consciousness). Inner life, James observed, alternates like a bird's life between "perches" (brief substantive resting places where an image or conclusion crystallizes) and "flights" (the rapid, transitive feelings of relation—and, if, but, by—that carry thought from one perch to the next).

Within three decades, modernist writers led by Dorothy Richardson, Virginia Woolf (Mrs. Dalloway, 1925), and James Joyce (Ulysses, 1922) turned James's psychological observation into the defining literary form of the twentieth century. Abandoning the omniscient Victorian narrator, they transcribed the raw, associative, unpunctuated interior monologue of characters across a single day in London or Dublin.

Yet when experimental psychologists finally designed rigorous tools to sample what actually happens inside ordinary people's heads from moment to moment, they discovered that human inner space is not a single species-wide design:

  • Congenital Aphantasia and Hyperphantasia (2015): Building on Galton's forgotten survey, neurologist Adam Zeman at the University of Exeter studied patients who lost visual imagery after cardiac surgery and soon identified thousands of healthy individuals born with aphantasia—the complete absence of voluntary visual imagery. In 2022, Joel Pearson and colleagues proved that aphantasia is not merely a difference in self-report: when ordinary subjects or hyperphantasics* are asked to imagine a blindingly bright white triangle in a dark room, their physical pupils automatically constrict as if bracing for glare, whereas the pupils of aphantasics show zero imaginary light response until taxed by working-memory load.
  • * Descriptive Experience Sampling of Inner Speech (1973–present): Beginning in the 1970s at the University of Nevada, psychologist Russell Hurlburt equipped volunteers with random pocket beepers that sounded at unpredictable intervals during daily life, training them to freeze and record the exact form of their experience at the microsecond of the beep. While literature and philosophy assumed all human thought is an unbroken verbal monologue, Hurlburt found that inner speech frequency ranges from nearly 100 percent in some adults to zero percent in others, who think entirely in unworded concepts, somatic feelings, or silent spatial schematics.
  • The Bicameral Mind Hypothesis (1976): Pushing the historical plasticity of inner experience to its furthest extreme, Princeton psychologist Julian Jaynes noted in The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind that characters in the earliest layers of Homer's Iliad* never sit down to deliberate inside an introspective mental space; instead, under acute decision stress, they hear the externalized voice of Athena or Apollo commanding them to act. Jaynes proposed that self-reflexive interior narration is a cultural technology forged only three thousand years ago when complex urban societies and written law forced the human brain to unify its hemispheric voices under a single metaphorical "I."
Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: After William James coined the stream of thought in 1890 and Virginia Woolf and James Joyce turned it into modernist literature in the 1920s, psychologist Russell Hurlburt's beeper sampling studies beginning in 1973 and neurologist Adam Zeman's 2015 identification of congenital aphantasia revealed radical hidden divergence in human inner experience, alongside Julian Jaynes's 1976 bicameral mind hypothesis.
Empirical Payoff: When congenital aphantasics and hyperphantasics are shown bright or dark triangles only in their imagination, ordinary subjects' pupils automatically constrict to imagined brightness while aphantasic pupils remain unchanged unless they are solving a heavy working-memory load.
