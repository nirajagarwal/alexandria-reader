Consciousness · Chapter 5

The Controlled Hallucination

Rubber Hands, Out-of-Body Switches, and How a Brain in a Dark Box Guesses the World

In the 1860s, German physicist and physician Hermann von Helmholtz realized something unsettling about human perception. Because your brain sits trapped inside a dark skull with no direct window to the outside world, it can never inspect reality directly. Instead, your brain has to work backward like a detective. It takes ambiguous electrical clues from the eyes and skin and guesses what object out in the world caused them.

Modern neuroscientists like Andy Clark, Karl Friston, and Anil Seth call the brain a prediction machine, and they describe normal waking consciousness as a controlled hallucination. Most people think vision works from the outside in, with the eyes sending a finished photograph up to the brain. In fact, inside the visual cortex, far more nerve cables run downward from the brain's higher memory centers toward the eyes than run upward from the eyes to the brain.

Your brain constantly broadcasts its best top-down guess of what it expects to see next. The eyes only send back an error report when reality disagrees with the guess. Mathematically, the brain updates its internal belief about a hidden cause $C$ given sensory data $D$ using Thomas Bayes's rule of probability:

$$P(C \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid C) \, P(C)}{P(D)}$$

Here $P(C)$ is your brain's prior expectation before looking, and $P(D \mid C)$ is how well the incoming sensory signals match that expectation. That is why two people could stare at the exact same viral photograph of a striped dress in 2015 and see completely different colors. If your brain unconsciously assumed the dress was standing in bluish daylight shadow, it subtracted the blue and showed you a white-and-gold dress. If your brain assumed warm indoor yellow light, it subtracted the yellow and showed you a blue-and-black dress.

Even more shocking is that your feeling of owning your own physical body is also a live guess that can be hacked in sixty seconds. In 1998 at the University of Pittsburgh, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen hid a volunteer's real left hand behind a cardboard divider. They placed a lifelike rubber left hand on the desk right in front of the volunteer. Then the researcher stroked both the hidden real hand and the visible rubber hand at the exact same rhythm with two small paintbrushes.

Within one minute, the brain merged the sight of the brush on the rubber hand with the feeling of the brush on the real skin. It reassigned ownership of the rubber hand to itself. When researchers suddenly pulled out a hammer and swung it at the rubber hand, volunteers flinched, gasped, and broke into a cold sweat as if their own flesh were about to be smashed.

In 2007 in Stockholm, neuroscientist Henrik Ehrsson took that trick to the whole body. Volunteers wore virtual-reality goggles fed by two cameras mounted six feet behind their own back, so they were looking at their own back from behind. When Ehrsson stood behind them and tapped their real chest with a rod while poking the empty air just below the camera lenses in sync, volunteers felt their conscious self jump six feet backward out of their own body. Even the feeling of sitting inside your own skin is a moment-by-moment construction of the brain.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: In 1998 at the University of Pittsburgh, psychiatrists Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen hid a volunteer's real left hand behind a cardboard screen, placed a lifelike rubber hand on the table in front of them, and stroked both hands in sync with two small paintbrushes for sixty seconds—tricking the brain into feeling the brush strokes inside the lifeless rubber fingers.
