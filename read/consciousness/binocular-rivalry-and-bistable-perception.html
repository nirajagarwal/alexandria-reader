Consciousness · Chapter 6

Binocular Rivalry and Bistable Perception

Where the Primate Visual Hierarchy Switches From Retinal Input to Subjective Percept

Place a vertical mirror reflection of the letter A before your left eye and a reflection of the letter S before your right eye using the mirror stereoscope invented by physicist Charles Wheatstone at King's College London in 1838. Because the two contours fall on corresponding retinal coordinates but cannot be fused into a single three-dimensional object, the visual system faces an irreconcilable conflict. Instead of perceiving a superimposed transparent jumble of both letters, your conscious sight alternates spontaneously every two to four seconds. First the A fills awareness while the S vanishes completely. Moments later, a wave of dominance sweeps across the visual field, erasing the A and revealing the S.

For consciousness researchers in the 1990s, Wheatstone's binocular rivalry offered an ideal experimental scalpel. Throughout a rivalry session, the physical photons striking both retinas remain 100 percent constant, yet the observer's conscious percept flips back and forth. Suppose a neurophysiologist records from single neurons along the primate visual pathway while a subject reports these alternations. Which brain areas loyally track the constant physical stimulus on the retina, and which areas flip in lockstep with subjective awareness?

Between 1989 and 1997, neuroscientist Nikos Logothetis and his colleagues Jeffrey Schall, David Leopold, and David Sheinberg at Baylor College of Medicine answered that question in awake behaving rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta). The monkeys were trained to pull left or right levers to report whether they perceived leftward-tilted gratings, rightward-tilted gratings, sunburst patterns, or faces presented separately to the two eyes. Using tungsten microelectrodes, Logothetis's team recorded extracellular action potentials from hundreds of individual neurons across four successive rungs of the macaque visual cortex.

The recordings revealed a steep hierarchical gradient across the cortex. In primary visual cortex (area V1), roughly eighty-two percent of orientation-selective neurons kept firing steadily to whichever grating hit their preferred eye. They remained completely indifferent to whether the monkey consciously saw that grating or the rival one; only eighteen percent modulated their firing rate with the monkey's reported percept. One rung higher, in intermediate visual areas V4 and MT, the fraction of percept-tracking neurons rose to roughly forty percent.

To model how competing cortical pools generate spontaneous dominance switches from steady retinal inputs $I_1$ and $I_2$, computational neuroscientists Hugh Wilson and Carson Chow formulated coupled rate equations. These equations govern the excitatory firing rates $E_1, E_2$ and slow spike-frequency adaptation currents $A_1, A_2$:

$$\tau \frac{dE_1}{dt} = -E_1 + \sigma\bigl(I_1 - \beta E_2 - g A_1\bigr), \qquad \tau_A \frac{dA_1}{dt} = -A_1 + E_1$$

Here, the dominant neuronal pool $E_1$ suppresses its rival $E_2$ through GABAergic cross-inhibition of strength $\beta$. While $E_1$ fires rapidly, however, slow calcium-dependent potassium channels build up the hyperpolarizing adaptation variable $A_1$ over a long time constant $\tau_A \gg \tau$. Once self-adaptation $g A_1$ weakens the dominant pool below the cross-inhibition threshold, the suppressed pool $E_2$ escapes inhibition and takes over awareness. This dynamical balance also explains the counterintuitive psychophysical law discovered by Dutch psychologist Willem Levelt in 1965. Increasing the physical contrast of the image shown to one eye does not lengthen how long that strong eye stays dominant. Instead, higher contrast shortens the dominance duration of the other eye by overcoming its inhibitory adaptation faster. At the top of the ventral visual stream, in the inferior temporal (IT) cortex and superior temporal sulcus, Sheinberg and Logothetis found in 1997 that ninety percent of responsive neurons fired vigorously when their preferred face or sunburst pattern dominated awareness. Those same inferior temporal neurons dropped to baseline firing rates the instant the rival image suppressed the preferred stimulus, even though both images remained focused on the retinas.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: Charles Wheatstone's 1838 Royal Society stereoscope paper, Willem Levelt's 1965 rivalry laws, and Nikos Logothetis, David Leopold, and David Sheinberg's 1996–1997 Nature and PNAS single-unit recordings at Baylor College of Medicine and the Max Planck Institute for Biological Cybernetics.
Empirical Payoff: When David Sheinberg and Nikos Logothetis recorded from single neurons in the inferior temporal cortex and superior temporal sulcus of awake rhesus macaques at Baylor College of Medicine in 1997 during flash-suppression and binocular rivalry trials, ninety percent of responsive neurons fired vigorously when their preferred face or sunburst pattern dominated awareness and dropped to baseline firing rates the instant the rival image suppressed it, even though both images remained focused on the retinas.
