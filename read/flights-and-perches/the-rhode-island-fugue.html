William James · Chapter 8

The Rhode Island Fugue

Ansel Bourne and the Clinical Discovery of the Subliminal Self

Five o'clock had barely struck on the morning of Monday, March 14, 1887, inside a small red-brick house and storefront at 252 East Main Street in Norristown, Pennsylvania. Suddenly, the tenant bolted upright in bed in terror. For the past six weeks, the quiet sexagenarian newcomer—known to his landlord Earhart family and neighborhood customers as Albert J. Brown—had operated a neat five-cent stationery, toy, and confectionery shop in the front room. He had stocked his shelves on weekly train trips to Philadelphia wholesalers, cooked his own meals in the back room, and spoken once at the local Methodist prayer meeting. Looking around the dark bedroom on that March morning, the man had no idea what town he was in. He did not know who owned the bed he was lying in, or how a candy store came to be attached to his doorway.

Knocking in panic on his landlord's door to ask the date and place, the terrified tenant insisted that his real name was Ansel Bourne. He explained that he was an itinerant evangelical preacher and former carpenter from the village of Greene, Rhode Island. The last event he could remember was stepping onto a horse-car in Providence on the afternoon of Monday, January 17, 1887—nearly two months earlier—after withdrawing $551 in cash from a bank in Pawtucket to pay for a farm lot. Thin, confused, and trembling, Bourne feared the local police would arrest him as a burglar who had broken into the store during the night. His nephew Andrew Harris soon arrived by train from Providence to settle the shop accounts and bring the shaken preacher home. Only then did the family realize that Ansel Bourne had lived two severed biographies across fifty-six days.

Orthodox nineteenth-century Anglo-American psychiatry had only two categories for such an episode. Either the patient was a conscious impostor feigning amnesia to escape debts, or he had suffered a prolonged epileptic automatism that left no organized memory trace behind. In January 1890, William James and his colleague Richard Hodgson of the American Society for Psychical Research invited the aging preacher to Cambridge. They wanted to test a third hypothesis pioneered by Pierre Janet at the Salpêtrière Hospital in Paris. Janet proposed that a human mind can split (dissociate) into two coherent, alternating streams of consciousness. Each stream possesses its own continuous memory chain while remaining hidden beneath the threshold of the other.

Across five days of clinical sessions in January 1890 and follow-up experiments that June, James induced a hypnotic trance by having Bourne gaze steadily at a blank sheet of writing paper. The instant Bourne's eyelids drooped into trance, the evangelical preacher vanished and the Norristown shopkeeper took command of his vocal cords:

  • * State-Dependent Identity Switching: Under hypnosis, the subject flatly denied being Ansel Bourne, professed ignorance of Rhode Island, and stated that his name was Albert J. Brown, born in Newton, New Hampshire, on July 8, 1826, adding that he had endured a great deal of trouble and was altogether a weak and broken man.
  • * Forensic Reconstruction of the Missing Eight Weeks: Speaking as A. J. Brown, the hypnotized man gave a day-by-day itinerary of how he had boarded a train from Pawtucket to Boston on January 17, 1887, traveled through New York to Philadelphia (staying two weeks at the Grand Central Hotel), leased the Norristown storefront from the Earhart family, and retired to bed normally on the night of March 13, 1887—every single detail of which Hodgson subsequently verified against hotel registers and wholesale receipts.
  • * Asymmetric Amnesia Barrier: While the waking Ansel Bourne remembered his entire sixty-one years up to January 17, 1887, and his life after waking on March 14, 1887, the hypnotic Albert J. Brown knew only the eight-week bridge between those two dates and regarded the preacher's body as an exhausted vessel.

James and Hodgson brought Bourne back to Norristown under hypnosis in June 1890 in hopes of fusing the two memory streams together. Despite their efforts, the barrier between the preacher and the shopkeeper held firm for the remaining twenty years of Bourne's life.

Primary Anchor & Verified Finding
Historical / Experimental Grounding: The case of sixty-one-year-old itinerant evangelical preacher Ansel Bourne of Greene, Rhode Island, who withdrew $551 from the Pawtucket bank on January 17, 1887, vanished for eight weeks, rented a shop in Norristown, Pennsylvania under the name 'Albert J. Brown,' and woke up terrified on March 14, 1887 with zero memory of the intervening two months; investigated by William James and Richard Hodgson across five hypnosis sessions in January and June 1890.
Empirical Payoff: Although James and Hodgson brings Bourne back to Norristown under hypnosis in June 1890 in hopes of fusing the two memory streams together, the barrier between the preacher and the shopkeeper held firm for the remaining twenty years of Bourne's life.
